Limits of Observability

ET-SoC-1, the AI Foundry cards on aifoundry2 and aifoundry3, et-platform 353f20e firmware, core-et b38a1a3 RTL · first edition 20 September 2026; second edition 23 September, with the power meters, the unmetered remainder, the improvement ladder and the index of measurement reports; updated 24 September with the heat-per-millimetre runs and two more limits of the meter · the hub for everything about measuring this card

This chip ships with its manuals, errata, firmware source, functional simulator and the RTL of its core. The question was: how far down can you see, and can you watch individual bits flip? The short answer has three parts. On the card, from a user account, the floor is one clock cycle for time and one 64-bit register of a halted hart for state; no counter, trace or sensor returns the value of an arbitrary flop or SRAM bit, and the only bit-level detectors, the ECC checkers, are wired but not switched on. In the functional simulator, every architectural bit every instruction, with no timing. In RTL simulation, every flop every cycle: single modules of the original RTL already run here under Verilator (one of them explains a counter bug measured on the card, §3), and the whole eight-minion bench needs more repair. Below silicon, this is far more than a GPU offers. On silicon, it is roughly a GPU's depth with weaker ECC coverage, and much of the extra reach sits behind firmware that the card's boot chain signature-checks. For energy, the floor is a watt-hour's worth of identical events: board power at 10 mW every 133 ms and three rails, which the energy manual turns into picojoules per instruction and per byte by repetition. Half of the idle power and a fifth of any workload's is on no rail sensor at all; §4 says what that remainder is made of and §5 what would meter it.

    3. Can we track individual bits flipping?

    On the card: no, and not with any firmware change. Nothing on the die counts toggles. The PMU counts operations (up to 8 per cycle per neighbourhood), the shire-cache and memory-controller monitors count requests, and the power path resolves 1 mW over 133 ms, twelve orders of magnitude above one node toggle. The closest things to a bit read are the ECC machinery and the debug module, and both stop at architectural state:

    • ECC covers the L2/L3/scratchpad SRAM and the instruction cache only. The L1 data cache, the tensor scratchpad carved from it, the integer register file and the 256-bit vector register file have no ECC and no parity, so a flip there is invisible to hardware. DRAM ECC is compiled off (ddr_mode.ecc=false). The SRAM error logger records which 64-bit doubleword of a line failed, not which bit. And at this firmware the service processor never enables the interrupt sources that carry SRAM errors, so SramCeEvent and DM_CMD_GET_MODULE_SRAM_ECC_UECC read 0 forever. (We read them: 0.)
    • The Minion Debug Interface reads state, not flips. The service processor can halt a neighbourhood, inject two-instruction pairs into a hart, and hand back any GPR, the PC, seven CSRs and 8-byte memory words. That is one architectural snapshot per millisecond-scale round trip. It is compiled into the shipped firmware and the management node is world-writable, but no installed tool sends the commands, halting mid-kernel wedges the kernel, single-step is a stub, and the boot code clock-gates the debug logic, so the first MDI command on this card is itself an experiment.
    • Raw SRAM rows with their check bits can be read and written through the shire cache's M-mode index-cacheop state machine (Dbg_Read/Dbg_Write, PRM Table 15-81). That is a literal bit read of a stored line, and a fault injector, but it needs a syscall the firmware does not have.
    • The UltraSoC debug fabric is on the die but unreachable. The RTL wires a per-minion instruction-trace encoder (retired instruction and PC every cycle), per-neighbourhood status monitors (200 filter, 64 match, 128 data signals, 3 counters) and a memory-shire monitor over 200 controller signals. The IP, its message-engine protocol and the Data Book are not open, no firmware line configures it, and the SP gates its clock at boot.
    • Physically: scan chains exist (a one-shot, stopped-clock flop dump with tooling that is not open), and laser or e-beam probing is a different project. Not for a shared PCIe card.

    A first result from the RTL. On the card, hpmcounter3 reads 128 short whenever its low 7 bits are 0–10. Simulating the original neigh_pmu.v under Verilator (rtl-sim/pmu_carry) shows why: each counter is a 7-bit pre-counter plus a 57-bit post-counter, twelve counters share one adder that folds pre-counter overflows into the post-counters in round-robin order, and a read ignores the pending overflow bit. So after every wrap the value is 128 short until the adder comes round again: 12 cycles in the simulation, 11 on the card. That is a silicon measurement explained by a flip-flop-level view of the design, which is the point of having the RTL.

    In simulation: yes, twice over. sys_emu -l prints every executed instruction on any selected hart with each architectural write it makes: integer registers, all 8 lanes of a vector register, mask registers, every memory read and write up to 512 bits, CSR writes, traps. XOR successive values and you have the bits that changed. It is functional, not cycle-accurate, and it models no cache contents. The RTL bench goes further: +dump=1 writes every net, flop and behavioural SRAM bit of the CPU subsystem to an FST waveform each cycle, and the design verification monitors emit a cycle-stamped event per retired instruction that is checked in lockstep against the same emulator. Toggle counts per signal are a coverage flag. What you cannot get from the drop is joules: there is no cell library, netlist or power flow, so toggles stay counts. The one place joules have been attached to flips is the tensor unit, by fitting four event energies to board power (the Horace experiment).

    4. Power and energy: the meter chain, and what it does not meter

    4.1 The chain

    The card's power is read by a PMIC on the board — a microcontroller with its own closed firmware — that measures the 12 V input and, through PMBus, the three digital regulators that feed the minion cores, the SRAM arrays and the mesh. For each of those three it holds voltage, current and power on both sides of the regulator, and its temperature, each as a current value, a minimum, a maximum and an average since the last reset: 84 numbers. The service processor reads all 84 every loop pass, keeps the output-side power of each as a first-order filtered average (τ ≈ 1 s: 61% of a step after one second, 88% after two) with the min and max, and forwards those with the input power to the host, where ettelem samples them at 10 Hz. The PMIC refreshes every 133 ms because the SP's I2C driver waits a millisecond after each of its transactions. The other rails — — have set-point registers in the PMIC and no telemetry at all.

    The chain has one more limit, found while making the manual's bars: the workload can starve the meter. Rings of messages between shires s and s+16 slow the service processor's own management path — each command takes 150 ms instead of 22, and the board reading stays frozen for seconds at a time — so a burst measured through it reads 45% low on aifoundry2. The sampler records its own latency with every sample, and bursts with that signature are dropped (rung 7). The heat-per-mm runs found a second pattern that does it — on aifoundry2, tensor loads between shires in the same column three hops apart take each command to about a second — and a way to stop the meter outright: a sampler killed in the middle of a request leaves its reply in the management queue, and every later opener crashes on that reply until one command drains it. The same runs sent about a second of stray tensor stores to physical address 0, through a bug in the test tool; no error counter, telemetry field or clock moved, so a wild write is one more thing the card does not report.

    The remainder cannot be metered with anything on the card, but it can be attributed. A DRAM byte's unmetered energy is the memory's: about 70 pJ per byte in the DDR PHY, the I/O rail and the DRAM chips, on top of the 50–60 pJ the mesh, the SRAM and the delivery losses take on the way; a byte written through the L1 costs twice that off-rail, because the line is read from DRAM before it is written. And the NoC coefficient is not all regulator: 26–29% is too much for a delivery loss, and the memory shires' own logic, on an unmetered rail, works whenever the mesh moves bytes to them. What the fit cannot say is how the idle 12–15 W splits between DDR refresh and PHY, PCIe, the IO shire, Maxion and the regulators' own draw, nor how the DRAM term splits below the regulator.

    4.3 What the Moortec sensors can and cannot do for power

    The die carries a Moortec PVT subsystem: five controllers, each with eight temperature sensors, two sixteen-channel voltage monitors and eight process detectors. Thirty-five temperature sensors are live (one per minion shire and one in the IO shire; the PCIe shire's was dropped from the RTL), at 12 bits and 0.061 °C. The voltage monitors sense 125 points at 14 bits (75 µV): three rails in every minion shire, two in every memory shire, three in the IO shire, two in the PCIe shire, and two external analog inputs. The process detectors are ring oscillators, one set per controller. The spatial temperature brief maps the sensors onto the 6×6 mesh and traces how the firmware collapses them to one average.

    None of it measures current, so none of it meters the unmetered rails. What the monitors do measure is voltage, and voltage droops with current. The host already receives, every 133 ms, the memory shires' reading of the 0.8 V DDR rail (DM_CMD_GET_ASIC_VOLTAGE, ettelem's die_mv.ddr): across the catalogue it sits mV at idle against an 800 mV set point, and under load it droops in proportion to the DRAM power the regression attributes off-rail:

    That is a current meter for the largest unmetered consumer, calibrated once, reading at 10 Hz with 1 mV ≈ 1.2 W, and it costs nothing: it has been in every telemetry file since the first session. It is not independent of the board meter — its scale comes from the fit above — but it responds to DRAM traffic alone, so it separates DRAM from everything else in a mixed burst and it does not drift with the die temperature the way the board's idle does. The same monitors see the minion rail sag by mV per watt the cores draw, which the spatial brief turned into a per-shire IR-drop map from the SP's debug trace; calibrated shire by shire, that map is a spatial power meter for the three metered rails (rung 5). The temperature sensors and the process detectors, exported, would add heat maps and a leakage proxy per shire; they too need a firmware change.

    5. The improvement ladder

    Every way found to see more, ordered by what it costs: first what the card already gives and only software is missing, then hardware the lab can add without touching the card's firmware, then firmware, then research. Each rung says what it adds and what it would change in the numbers. Rungs marked done are in the energy manual or on this page. The firmware caveat at the end of the section applies to every rung in its group.

    What each rung does to the unmetered watts. Today: 15 W of a 32 W idle and 4–7 W of a DRAM workload are on no sensor, and the regression names them to 0.35 W (rungs 2 and 3, done). The riser (rung 8) does not meter them either, but it makes every bracketed burst sharper and shows the input side of every regulator's transient. The camera (rung 9) says which package the watts warm. Forwarding the PMIC's input-side readings (rung 11) measures the delivery losses and leaves only the rails with no meter — DDR, VDDQ, PCIe, IO, Maxion — as a remainder, about 12 W at idle. Below that is the board, and the board is shared.

    The firmware caveat. Every "firmware" row above ends with a reflash. The minion runtime and the service processor bootloader are signed executable images; BL1 and BL2 verify a certificate and signature against a public-key hash in OTP unless a chicken bit is set, and neither the signing tool nor a test key is in the open tree. Whether this card accepts a rebuilt image is unknown until someone tries, and a bad image can brick the boot path, so it is a lab-admin decision. This is the one place where "everything is open" has a gap: the source is open, the right to run modified source on this particular card is not yet established.

    6. Against a GPU

    For an A100/H100-class part. Sources are NVIDIA's own documentation and the microbenchmark literature; the ET side is this survey.

    The pattern: the GPU is at least as observable on silicon (ECC counts, a debugger, SASS-level instrumentation, a mJ energy counter the ET card lacks), and entirely opaque below it. The ET-SoC-1 is thinner on silicon (no L1 or register ECC, DRAM ECC off, power at 133 ms) and open all the way down: the firmware that decides what you can see is source, the reference model the RTL was verified against is the installed simulator, and the RTL of the core dumps every bit.

      8. Method and caveats

      • Seven scouts each surveyed one layer of the manuals (external/et-man), firmware at the card's commit (external/et-platform at 353f20e, checked against HEAD), the RTL and DV trees (external/core-et), the installed tools in /opt/et, and the earlier reports. Each key claim was then handed to two independent reviewers told to refute it, one checking citations and one checking whether it would work from a user account on this card. The ladder folds in their corrections; rows they did not reach are marked ·. The raw findings and verdicts are in docs/reports/sources/2026-09-20-limits-of-observability/.
      • The second edition's power section reads the PMIC and PVT drivers of the same firmware (ServiceProcessorBL2/driver/pmic_controller.c, pvt_controller.c, services/thermal_pwr_mgmt.c) and fits the energy manual's catalogue (docs/reports/data/2026-09-23-catalogue-aifoundry2/, -aifoundry3/); the fit and the droop calibration are in docs/reports/data/2026-09-23-energy-manual/unmetered_fit.json. Those rows have not had the two-reviewer pass.
      • Nothing here changed the card. The only measurements taken for this page were reads of the ECC counters and sysfs error statistics, and the reports' own timing and power runs.
      • The RTL is the Erbium branch: the same Minion core lineage as the ET-SoC-1, but a later MCU-class configuration (one neighbourhood, no shire cache), with known deviations. The PRM and errata stay authoritative for the A0 silicon. The same repository's main branch holds a clean-room, Verilator-first re-implementation including a shire cache, with toggle coverage in CI; it is a re-implementation, not the taped-out RTL.
      • Several rows rest on a single reading of the sources and were not exercised: MDI on this card, the debug clock gate, the memory-shire perfmon type codes, the per-shire LVDPLL (which an erratum calls unstable on A0), the external analog monitor inputs. They are labelled accordingly.
