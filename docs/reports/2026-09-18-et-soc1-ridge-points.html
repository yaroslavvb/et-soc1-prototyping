Ridge points: how much reuse the ET-SoC-1 needs at each level of memory

Computed 18 Sep 2026 from existing data, with no new runs. Compute peaks come from the Minion VPU Specification. Bandwidths were measured on the lab cards aifoundry2 and aifoundry3 (1,024 compute minions; minion clock 600 MHz, NoC 400 MHz, DDR 933 MHz). Spec values come from the manuals and micro-architecture docs, with sources at the end. Code: scripts/ridge-points.py in the nekko workspace. Part of the ET-SoC-1 measurement reports.

A roofline ridge point is the arithmetic intensity at which a kernel stops waiting for memory: peak compute divided by the bandwidth of the level the data comes from. Below it, that level sets the speed; above it, the arithmetic units do. For the ET-SoC-1's tensor unit at 600 MHz, fp32 work needs about 4 FLOP per byte loaded from the minion's own shire (its L2 cache or L2 scratchpad), 10 per byte from L3 or another shire's scratchpad, 130 per byte from DRAM, and about 620 per byte from host memory over PCIe. fp16 needs twice as much and int8 eight times, because the tensor unit does 2× and 8× as many fp16 and int8 operations per cycle. One full-size TensorFMA already does 4 FLOP per byte it loads in fp32 (8 in fp16), so tiles held in the shire can just keep fp32 and fp16 busy, with no margin. Beyond the shire, and above all from DRAM, every byte has to be reused many times. From DRAM that takes C blocks of about 520 × 520 in fp32 and fp16, and about 1,040 × 1,040 in int8.

The compute ceilings

Each minion's vector unit has 8 lanes. Each lane has one FMA unit that does one fp32 or two fp16 multiply-adds per cycle, and two integer units that do four int8 multiply-adds each. A tensor instruction keeps those units busy for 512 cycles (fp32 and fp16) or 256 cycles (int8) per 16×16×K op, so a minion peaks at 16 fp32 FLOP, 32 fp16 FLOP or 128 int8 OP per cycle. The int8 rate is 8× fp32, not 4×, because the integer units are separate and there are twice as many. Only hart 0 of a minion may issue tensor ops, and the vector unit's fmadd.ps runs on the same FMA units. So hart 1 and the vector unit add nothing to these peaks.

Ridge points by level

Bandwidths are given per minion per minion-clock cycle, as a kernel running on all 1,024 minions sees them, and for the whole chip at 600 MHz. Measured is the median streaming rate of the earlier reports' probes with every minion reading its own data. Bytes per cycle come from the cycle counters, and GB/s from the 600 MHz launches only. So the chip figures are lower than the memory-hierarchy report's table (L2 2.80, local scratchpad 2.57, L3 1.03 TB/s), which averaged launches at 600–800 MHz. By the cycle counters, that report's "about 144 B/cycle per shire" for L2 is 128. Spec is what the documented port and bank widths allow. Ridge = peak per minion-cycle ÷ bytes per minion-cycle.

Ridge points in FLOP (int8: OP) per byte at 600 MHz. Bandwidth: bytes per minion per cycle · the whole chip. For TensorSend rows, "bytes" are bytes one minion sends another.

  • Inside a minion, nothing binds. The tensor unit reads its A operand from the L1 scratchpad at one request of at most 32 B per cycle. With B streamed through TenB, a full-size op keeps that port 50–75% busy. (In int8 with B also in the scratchpad it reaches 88%, and the op slows from 280 to 318 cycles.) fp32 and fp16 keep C in the vector registers, whose three 32 B read ports per cycle are sized for one three-operand instruction per cycle. int8 accumulates in the separate 1 KB TenC.
  • Own shire. The probes stream 1 KB TensorLoads, two in flight per minion. With all 1,024 minions reading their own data, they level off at 4.0 B per cycle each (128 B per cycle per shire), from the L2 cache and the scratchpad alike, on aifoundry2. On aifoundry3 the scratchpad matched, but a shorter L2 probe reached only 3.3–3.5 B per cycle. The shire's four cache banks can return 256 B per cycle, twice the 4.0 rate. The int8 matmul loop consumed 7.3 B per cycle per minion, but all 1,024 minions read the same 32 KB of tiles, so some lines may have come from the L2's read buffers rather than its banks. So 4 FLOP/B is what a simple streaming kernel gets, and no kernel reading private data has yet approached the banks' spec ridge of 2.
  • Other shires. L3 lines are spread over all 32 shires, so almost every L3 hit crosses the mesh, just like a read from another shire's scratchpad. Both measured 0.96–0.98 TB/s, or 1.6 B per minion-cycle. The remote rate is for one pattern: each shire reading the shire 16 IDs away, 2.1 hops on average. If many shires read one shire's scratchpad, that shire's four mesh ports would allow only about 0.1 TB/s. At the NoC's 400 MHz, the ports of all 32 shires together would allow about 3.3 TB/s if each moved one 64 B beat per NoC cycle. No document states that rate for the shire ports; the datasheet's 32 GB/s per memory-shire NoC port is one 64 B beat per cycle at the 500 MHz design NoC clock. The mesh links, whose width is undocumented, could limit traffic spread over all shires first.
  • DRAM. Measured at 76 GB/s on aifoundry2 and 72 GB/s on aifoundry3. For comparison, 16 channels give 119 GB/s at the 3,733 MT/s implied by the firmware's 933 MHz DDR clock, and 136.5 GB/s at the datasheet maximum of 4,266 MT/s. The datasheet prints 133 Gbytes/s, which is 136,512 MB/s divided by 1,024. The 128,000 MB/s the runtime reports is a constant in the service processor's firmware, not a measurement. A third party estimated about 88 GB/s as achievable on another card. That figure was calculated from the best rates measured against single memory shires, not measured for the whole chip.
  • Host memory. PCIe Gen4 x8 carries 15.75 GB/s per direction before protocol overhead. Host transfers have not been timed on these cards.
  • TensorSend moves data between minions' registers rather than fetching it, and each rate belongs to the pattern measured: pairs, rings, or shire to shire. It is not the capacity of a link. Across the mesh, a shire manages only 3–5 GB/s, limited by how many packets a sender keeps in flight.

Which ridge points move with the clock

Levels inside the shire run on the minion clock, because the shire cache is clocked with the minions. On aifoundry2, whose governor moved the clock between 600 and about 800 MHz from launch to launch, the L2 and scratchpad bandwidths rose exactly with the clock. Their ridge points therefore hold at any clock. L3 and remote scratchpad reads go through the NoC, which the firmware keeps at 400 MHz. Over launches up to about 740 MHz, their bandwidth rose only 0.65× and 0.33× as fast as the minion clock. DRAM bandwidth did not change at all, and neither will PCIe, so their ridge points grow in proportion to the minion clock.

fp16 is 2× and int8 8× every entry. Only the minion clock changes. The NoC stays at the lab cards' 400 MHz and DDR at 933 MHz; at the NoC's 500 MHz design clock the L3 and remote-scratchpad ridges would be lower. For those two, the lower end assumes their bandwidth keeps rising with the clock as it did between 600 and about 740 MHz, and the upper end assumes it stays fixed.

What it takes to reach them

Matrix multiplication

  • One op. A 16×16×K TensorFMA reads a 1 KB A tile from the L1 scratchpad and a 1 KB B tile streamed through TenB. It does 8,192 FLOP in fp32, 16,384 in fp16 or 32,768 OP in int8. With both tiles loaded fresh each time, that is 4, 8 or 16 per byte: exactly the own-shire ridge for fp32 and fp16, and half of it for int8. The matmul benchmark ran fp32 and fp16 at 97% of peak and int8 at 91% with tiles in L2. But in all three runs every minion cycled through the same 32 KB tile pool, so those rates do not show that private tiles streamed from the banks would keep the tensor unit busy. A kernel with its own tiles in the shire sits exactly at the fp32 and fp16 ridge, with no margin.
  • The shire. The 32 minions of a shire can share loads. A cooperative TensorLoad reads a line once and delivers it to every minion in a group. Take a 4 × 8 grid of 16×16 C tiles, with A shared by the 8 minions of a neighbourhood and B across the 4 neighbourhoods. A minion-op then costs 384 B of bank reads instead of 2 KB, which is 21, 43 and 85 operations per byte, well past the spec ridge of 2, 4 and 16. Cooperative loads have not been measured on these cards.
  • Beyond the shire. A block of m × n outputs held in a shire while K streams past does 2mnK operations for every s·K·(m + n) bytes it reads, where s is the element size in bytes. Its intensity is H/s, where H = 2mn/(m + n) is the harmonic mean of m and n. The shire's 32 register-resident tiles form a 64 × 128 block with H = 85, which gives 21, 43 and 85 per byte against L3 or another shire. That beats the measured 10, 20 and 80, with little to spare in int8. It assumes each byte enters the shire once. Cooperative loads can do that for another shire's scratchpad, but they cannot read L3-homed addresses, so L3 data must be captured by the shire's L2 or copied into its scratchpad first. Likewise, the DRAM figures below assume each DRAM byte is read once for the whole chip.
  • DRAM. The block needs H ≥ s × ridge: about 520 in fp32 and fp16, and about 1,040 in int8. All 1,024 register tiles together form a 512 × 512 block (1 MB of C), which gives 128, 256 and 512 per byte. That is 99% of what fp32 and fp16 need and half of what int8 needs. Larger blocks have to keep C in the scratchpads and swap it through the registers. The 80 MB of scratchpad (the default partition) would hold C for a 4,500 × 4,500 fp32 block, but nobody has measured what the swapping costs. int8 is harder: its accumulator, TenC, cannot be loaded from memory, so partial sums would have to be added with vector instructions.

Inference that streams its weights

A layer that reads its weights once per batch of B inputs does 2B operations per weight, or 2B/s per byte. It becomes compute-bound when that reaches the ridge point, so it needs a batch of at least s × ridge / 2:

Weights come fromfp32 weightsfp16 weightsint8 weights

These are the smallest batches that are compute-bound at the measured bandwidth. The own-shire row also counts the activations. A minion holds one 16×16 C tile, so each op loads a fresh activation tile as well as a weight tile. Its intensity therefore tops out at 4, 8 and 16 per byte at batch 16, and int8 never reaches its 32 OP/B own-shire ridge. The other rows assume the activations stay in the shire and each weight byte reaches the shire once. On A0 silicon, fp32 and fp16 ops of 1–4 rows with B from TenB must be padded to 5 rows (errata 1.29 type D).

At batch 1 a layer does 0.5 FLOP per byte in fp32, so it is memory-bound at every level. The sparsity report's batch-1 layer, with its weights in the scratchpads and its partial sums added by the host, ran at 1.24 TFLOP/s (6.8 µs per 1024 × 4096 layer). That is the scratchpad bandwidth times 0.5, within 1%. With the on-chip reduction the layer takes 7.3 µs, 1.15 TFLOP/s.

Other limits that act like ridge points

  • A lone minion. A minion keeps at most 4 line requests in flight, shared by its two TensorLoad paths, so its rate is 4 lines per round trip. Streaming its own data alone, one minion measured 6.4 B per cycle from L2 or the scratchpad and 1.4 from DRAM. This is not a hard ceiling: the int8 matmul loop drew 7.3 B per cycle per minion. It takes at least 86–90 minions streaming 1 KB loads at once to reach the chip's 72–76 GB/s from DRAM, and more once queueing raises the latency.
  • Small loads. Every TensorLoad costs at least about 45 cycles, even with every line masked off, and about 65 for up to 4 lines when all minions load at once. So loads of fewer than 4 lines cannot reach the 4 B per cycle streaming rate: a 1-line load gets about 1.0 B per cycle. 4-line loads still reach 3.9.
  • Kernel launch. In the matmul runs, a launch from the host took 0.43 ms (median; 0.35–0.69 ms). That is as long as 4.2 GFLOP takes at the fp32 peak, so a kernel with less work than that spends more time launching than computing.
  • The vector unit's limit is not pinned down. A flw.ps loads 32 B and an fmadd.ps does 16 FLOP, and a minion issues at most one instruction per cycle for its two harts together. So with I FLOP per loaded byte, at most a fraction 2I/(2I + 1) of the issue slots can be FMAs. Measured code stays well below that bound. With both harts doing nothing but loading, the L1 delivered 10 B per minion-cycle, one flw.ps every 3.2 cycles, so below about 1.6 FLOP per byte the load rate binds first. The best published vector matmul has 63% FMAs in its instruction mix, yet reaches only 4.4 FLOP per minion-cycle, 28% of peak: it issues about 0.44 instructions per cycle. The spec ridges against the L1 cache (0.5 FLOP/B, 32 B per cycle) and the registers (0.17, 96 B per cycle) are never reached.

Energy balance points

Time is one budget and energy is another. Divide each level's energy per byte by the tensor unit's energy per FLOP, both measured as card power above idle. The result is the intensity at which moving the data costs as much energy as the arithmetic. With its operands already in the L1 scratchpad, the fp32 tensor unit costs 1.88 pJ per FLOP.

Where the bytes come frompJ per bytefp32 energy balance (FLOP/B)int8 energy balance (OP/B)fp32 time ridge (FLOP/B)

Energy per byte for memory is from the memory-hierarchy report, and for TensorSend from the on-chip communication report. The int8 column divides by the int8 matmul's 0.43 pJ per OP. That figure includes feeding the tiles from L2, so it overstates the arithmetic alone. The memory runs' higher clock pushes the other way, so the int8 column is not a strict bound either way.

At the measured bandwidths every fp32 energy balance point lies below the matching time ridge point. So a kernel that is compute-bound at those rates already spends more energy on arithmetic than on moving data. That does not hold at the spec rates: the L2 cache's 2.3 FLOP/B is above its spec ridge of 2.0, and L3's 5.7 is above 3.0. The two sides also come from different runs. The memory energy per byte averages every launch of the memory-hierarchy runs (implied minion clock 0.62–0.74 GHz, about 12 W above idle for busy cores alone). The tensor unit's pJ per FLOP comes from aifoundry3 at a steady 600 MHz (1.7 W for busy cores). So the memory rows overstate the energy per byte, and a real kernel pays the busy-chip floor only once. Treat these as rough.

Ridge points against sustained bandwidth, each ET-SoC-1 level next to its closest A100 counterpart. The A100's peaks are 19.5 TFLOP/s fp32 on the CUDA cores, 312 TFLOP/s fp16 and 624 TOP/s int8 on the tensor cores (dense). Its bandwidths are the sustained values in docs/reports/sources/2026-09-18-a100-memory-hierarchy.md (HBM2 1.40 TB/s; the memory-hierarchy report quotes 1.41). The ET-SoC-1's fp32 runs on its tensor unit.

The A100 has much more bandwidth, but also far more tensor throughput, so on chip its fp16 and int8 ridge points are higher. In fp16 it needs 21 FLOP per byte from shared memory, against the ET-SoC-1's 8 from its own shire. From L2 it needs 71, against the ET-SoC-1's 20 from L3 or another shire. From DRAM the fp16 ridge points are close: 223 from HBM, against 259 from LPDDR4X. In int8, though, the ET-SoC-1 needs 2.3× more (1,036 against 446). In IEEE fp32, which the A100 runs on its CUDA cores, its ridge points stay low throughout (14 from HBM), and the ET-SoC-1's DRAM ridge point is about 9× higher. With fp32 inputs rounded to TF32 on its tensor cores (156 TFLOP/s dense), the A100's HBM ridge would be about 111.

Caveats

  • The measured bandwidths are what the probes reached with 1 KB TensorLoads, two in flight. The spec values are what the documented port and bank widths allow. Neither is a proven ceiling. The spec figure for the mesh assumes one 64 B beat per NoC cycle per port.
  • These are read ridge points. No write bandwidth has been measured at any level, so a kernel that writes as much as it reads needs more.
  • All bandwidths are at a 600 MHz minion clock, a 400 MHz NoC and 933 MHz DDR. On aifoundry2 the governor moved the minion clock during the memory-hierarchy runs, so for levels beyond the shire only its 600 MHz launches were used. The energy per byte averages all of those runs' launches (see Energy balance points).
  • Two cards were used: aifoundry2 for the memory hierarchy, the matmul benchmark and messaging, and aifoundry3 for sparsity. The scratchpad rate matched on both; the L2 rate was lower on aifoundry3's shorter probe.
  • The port widths come from the core-et (erbium branch) documentation and RTL. A0 silicon may differ in details.
