ET-SoC-1 matmul on silicon: 91–97% of the tensor unit's peak, and its energy against the A100

18 September 2026 · aifoundry2: one ET-SoC-1 PCIe card, 32 GB LPDDR4X, minions at 600 MHz · code: kernels/mmbench in the project repository · part of the ET-SoC-1 measurement reports

A tensor-unit matmul kernel on all 1,024 compute minions sustains 9.5 TFLOP/s in fp32, 19.0 TFLOP/s with fp16 inputs and 71.8 TOP/s in int8. That is 91–97% of the tensor unit's peak at this clock, and every result is checked bit-exact against a host reference. The card draws 57–62 W doing it, against 30.6 W at idle with the die at 71 °C. Compared with the A100's spec sheet (peak ÷ TDP), the ET-SoC-1 is 3.4× more energy-efficient at full fp32 precision on these ±1/⁠±2 operands (about 2.9× in a later random-data measurement of the tensor unit at 80 °C). At fp16 and int8 the A100's tensor cores win on efficiency (2.4× and 1.3×) and by a much wider margin on raw speed (16× and 8.7×).

Later measurements. These operands favour low power. Every input is ±1 or ±2, whose mantissa bits are all zero, and the die was at 71–77 °C. The Horace experiment later showed how much the data matters: with the tensor unit busy on all 1,024 minions at 80 °C, the card draws 38 W on zeros, 47 W on ones, 51–53 W when only the signs or only the exponents are random, and 63 W on random-normal values. Random mantissas are the costliest bits.

In a variant of this loop with both operands in the L1 scratchpad (546 cycles per op rather than 529), random-normal fp32 at 80 °C ran at 9.18 TFLOP/s and 63.9 W. That is 144 GFLOP/s per W, about 2.9× the A100 SXM4's fp32 spec ratio rather than 3.4×; fp16 gave 301 GFLOP/s per W (energy manual §3.2).

Why is the ET-SoC-1 low power? sets this card against the A100's bf16 tensor cores instead, as measured by Horace He, and finds the A100 5.4× more efficient per FLOP than this card's fp32 and 2.6× more than its fp16. The 3.4× here is fp32 against the A100's fp32 CUDA-core datasheet figure; against its tensor cores the A100 table below gives 0.41–0.75×.

Terms. The ET-SoC-1's compute cores are minions: small in-order RISC-V cores, 32 to a shire; the 32 shires that run kernels hold 1,024 of them. Each minion has two hardware threads (harts) and a tensor unit whose matrix multiply-accumulate instruction, TensorFMA, is what this benchmark times. Each shire's 4 MB of SRAM includes a 512 KB L2 cache, where the compute-bound runs keep their tiles, and each minion sets aside 3 KB of its 4 KB L1 data cache as an L1 scratchpad for tensor operands. How the benchmark works

  • The loop. On each of the 1,024 compute minions, hart 0 (the first of the minion's two hardware threads) runs the loop. Only hart 0 may issue TensorFMA and TensorLoads into the L1 scratchpad, so hart 1 idles. Each op issues a tensor_load of a 16×K A tile into one of two L1-scratchpad buffers, a tensor_load of the B tile through TenB (the tensor unit's path that streams the B operand from memory), a tensor_wait, and a tensor_fma that accumulates a 16×16 C tile in the vector registers. The next op's loads overlap the running FMA. This is the inner loop of gp-sdk's auto-generated matmul and of the pipelined kernel in the FOSDEM 2026 "Zero to matmul" talk (10.25 TFLOP/s at 650 MHz).
  • Op size. One op is 16×16×K multiply-adds, with K = 16 (fp32), 32 (fp16) or 64 (int8). Operand layouts come from sw-sysemu's tensor emulation, which serves as the spec.
  • Working set. In the compute-bound runs all minions share 16 A/B tile pairs (32 KB), so the data stays in each shire's L2 cache. In the DRAM run each minion has its own 64 tile pairs (128 MB in total), so every load goes to memory. Effective read bandwidth there was about 78 GB/s.
  • Inputs and checking. Inputs are random values from {−2, −1, 1, 2}. They are never zero, so no multiply can be skipped, and they are exact in fp16 and fp32. The host checks each minion's C tile for exact equality with iters × Σ A·B, capping iteration counts so fp32 accumulation stays exact. Every launch passed with zero bad minions. Small powers of two keep the multipliers' mantissa bits at zero, so these operands draw less power than random data would.
  • Power. Board power comes from the card's own telemetry (DM_CMD_GET_MODULE_POWER through dev_mngt_service, about 7 samples/s). It is averaged over the launch windows after skipping the first second. The idle row is the median over the 8 s before the first workload; the above-idle column uses the idle just before each workload instead.

Caveats

  • The A100 figures are spec-sheet numbers, not measurements. The one measured A100 matmul these reports use (Horace He: bf16, random data, 257 TFLOPS under a 330 W limit) matches peak ÷ TDP. For the tensor cores these ratios are therefore a fair estimate, not a best case; no A100 fp32 CUDA-core GEMM was measured. Like for like on random data, this card's fp16 TensorFMA costs 3.3 pJ per FLOP at the board (energy manual §3.2, 80 °C) against the A100's 1.3 pJ in bf16, 2.6×, close to the 2.4× above.
  • Board power includes the whole card. Of the 30.6 W idle, about 12.6 W is fixed (DRAM, PCIe, I/O, regulators) and the rest is die leakage, which grows with temperature. The idle law in The DVFS loop and its leakage predicts 30.8 W at the 71 °C this run started at and 34.0 W at 77 °C. The above-idle column still carries the leakage added as the die warms within each run, so it is not the compute alone.
  • One clock point. This run stayed at 600 MHz (0.52 V) because the die started at 71 °C: aifoundry2's governor steps the clock down whenever the die reads above 65 °C, and raises it to 700 or 800 MHz (0.57 or 0.62 V) only while the die reads 65 °C or less (The DVFS loop and its leakage). Other operating points change both speed and efficiency. Power rose about 3 W within each 12 s run as the chip warmed, so longer runs would read slightly worse.
  • Precision differs. ET's fp32 tensor op is a full IEEE fp32 multiply-add, whose A100 equivalent is fp32 on CUDA cores; tf32 keeps only a 10-bit mantissa. ET's fp16 op accumulates in fp32 with round-toward-zero.
  • Peak-rate microbenchmark. This measures the matmul engine on cached tiles. A full GEMM also pays for moving data, and the DRAM row (0.31 TFLOP/s) is the no-reuse floor.

Setup notes (for running it yourself)

These notes are for running the benchmark on a lab machine, whose /opt/et is older than the gp-sdk that upstream documents. The laptop setup and the first scalar SGEMM, from the same day, are in Test drive.

  1. Access. I logged in over Tailscale SSH as a personal non-root account. It had to be created on aifoundry2 first: Tailscale SSH rejects a user that doesn't exist on the machine with the same "tailnet policy does not permit" message it gives for a made-up name. The machine runs x86-64 Ubuntu 24.04 with one card (/dev/et0_ops, /dev/et0_mgmt): PCIe Gen4, 32 GB, minion shires at 600 MHz and NoC at 400 MHz. Firmware is BL 0.20 and minion 0.23.
  2. Toolchain was already there. /opt/et is et-platform built from commit 353f20e (30 Dec 2025): RISC-V GCC 15.1, sys_emu, runtime, firmware and the upstream test binaries. It is not on PATH by default.
  3. Hello world as defined in et-platform's README (it_test_code_loading): 3/3 tests pass on the simulator in 110 s, about 34 s per test, almost all of it simulated firmware boot. The same binary with --mode=pcie passes 3/3 on the card in 0.6 s.
  4. et-testdrive (marty1885's minimal host+kernel project) builds unmodified against /opt/et. On the card all 64 harts of shire 0 print "Hello World from hart N", decoded in-process from the trace buffer. One catch: it opens the management node, which only one process may hold, so it fails with "Device or resource busy" while et-powertop is running. The upstream tests open only the ops node.
  5. Writing my own kernels with gp-sdk needed four fixes against this older install. All four are now in patches/lab-gp-sdk-06605ab.patch, which scripts/deploy-lab-gpsdk.sh applies:
    • Current gp-sdk requires et-common-libs' Erbium components, which the Dec 2025 install doesn't have. I pinned gp-sdk to 06605ab (24 Mar 2026, the last version before the Erbium changes) and made dnnLibrary and FFTW optional. Only the pre-generated matmul and the FFT example use them.
    • gp-sdk's GenericLauncher started sys_emu without firmware ELF paths. The simulated service processor then trapped at address 0 in a loop and wrote gigabytes of log within minutes (15 GB in 10 minutes on aifoundry2) before I stopped it and deleted the log. The repository's patches/et-platform-0002 (preload the firmware) fixes it.
    • Every gp-sdk kernel, including gp-sdk's own print.elf, died on launch with a U-mode instruction access fault at PC 0x40. Kernels are linked at address 0 and relocated by the runtime, but this gp-sdk links without -Wl,--emit-relocs, so the entry-point table kept its link-time address. Upstream restored the flag in 7e3b4c2 (2 May 2026), and I added it locally.
    • The old riscv_helpers.cmake uses CMake's plain target_link_libraries signature, which clashes with the keyword form this repository's kernels/CMakeLists.txt uses. I switched it to the keyword form, as upstream later did.
  6. Wrote the benchmark: kernels/mmbench (device), launchers/mmbench (host, checks every result), scripts/et-power-log.sh (board-power sampler) and scripts/mmbench-power.py (runs everything and averages power over the launch windows). It passed exact checks on the simulator on one shire in all modes, then on the card on all 32 shires, before the timed runs.

Reproduce

# hello world from et-platform's README
/opt/et/bin/it_test_code_loading               # simulator
/opt/et/bin/it_test_code_loading --mode=pcie   # the card

# benchmark: from a checkout of this repository, copy it to the lab machine
# and build gp-sdk (pinned and patched) and mmbench there
scripts/deploy-lab-gpsdk.sh aifoundry2
# then on aifoundry2, in the copy the script made at ~/nekko
# (quit et-powertop first: the power logger needs the management node)
cd ~/nekko && nice make mmbench-check bench-power DEVICE=silicon JOBS=4

The lab cards are shared, and the lab rule is to hold a device for at most 10 s. The run above kept the card busy for about 12 s per workload, before the project adopted that rule. scripts/mmbench-power.py now runs every launcher under timeout 10 and defaults to about 6 s per workload, which still leaves roughly 35 power samples per workload after the settle time.

Raw data: docs/reports/data/2026-09-18-aifoundry2 (board-power samples, one record per launch and the per-workload summary). scripts/mmbench-report-data.py turns it into this page's throughput, efficiency, idle before each workload (the same window as idle_before() in scripts/mmbench-power.py) and power chart; the tables are written by hand from its output.
