Sparsity on the ET-SoC-1: what the silicon skips, and where it can beat an A100

Measured 18 Sep 2026 on aifoundry3 in the AI Foundry lab: one ET-SoC-1 PCIe card, 1,024 compute minions, minion clock a steady 600 MHz in every sample. A100 values are published measurements, or estimates marked as such (sources at the end). Code: workloads/sparsity in the nekko workspace. Part of the ET-SoC-1 measurement reports.

The question was whether sparse or irregular compute favours this chip over an A100, and where. The tensor unit's zero-skip never saves time, but it saves most of the tensor unit's power. A 16×16×16 fp32 TensorFMA takes 546 cycles whether A is dense or all zeros. The fp16 (546), int8 (318) and row-masked (544) ops are just as flat. Board power above idle, though, falls from 17.3 W to 2.4 W as A goes from dense to all zeros in a loop of TensorFMAs. In a real layer, where loads dominate, zeros alone save far less. Speed has to come from software: masked loads that fetch only the rows a nonzero activation needs, and skipping zero tiles outright. With both, a batch-1 1024×4096 fp32 layer held in the on-chip scratchpads goes from 7.5 µs dense (7.3 µs with plain loads) to 2.1 µs at 99% zero activations, including the on-chip reduction. That is about the A100's latency, not a multiple of it. The chip should win where a GPU is stuck at its per-kernel floor and the state fits in SRAM: event-driven simulation, batch-1 pipelines of many small layers, fine-grained wavefronts. It loses wherever the sparse data has to stream from DRAM, and, against a modelled A100, on divergence alone.

So the gating works, and it is worth a lot of energy. A dense op costs 3.8 pJ per multiply slot above idle. An all-zero op still costs 0.5 pJ per slot, but most of that is the harts simply running: an integer loop on the same harts draws 1.7 of those 2.4 W. The tensor unit itself adds about 0.6 W with all zeros (0.14 pJ per slot), against 15.6 W dense. In this loop about 86% of the op's dynamic power follows the nonzero count. But no zero is ever faster than a nonzero. A kernel that wants time back from sparsity has to issue fewer or smaller TensorFMAs: skip all-zero tiles, or shrink AROWS and ACOLS. (On A0 silicon, errata 1.29 type D rules out AROWS = 3 with B in the scratchpad, and AROWS ≤ 3 with B from TenB, whenever ACOLS > 0.) The tensor unit will not do it by itself.

Masked loads save time, except when the chip saturates DRAM

A TensorLoad moves up to 16 lines of 64 B into the L1 scratchpad. With tensor_mask set, a masked line should never be requested. On one minion the time falls with the lines requested. From L2 and the shire's scratchpad it falls until a floor of about 45–47 cycles, the load's latency. From DRAM it falls almost in proportion, from 730 cycles for 16 lines to 73 for one: a lone minion's requests go out at about 45 cycles per line, far below what DRAM can deliver. (The DRAM buffer is 64 MB, larger than the 32 MB L3, so every load misses the caches.)

Cycles per 16-line TensorLoad against the lines the mask lets through, one minion (log scale)
DRAML2 (8 KB per minion)local L2 scratchpad

With all 1,024 minions loading at once, on-chip memory behaves the same way. DRAM does not. At 16 lines per load the chip streams 72 GB/s from DRAM, close to the 76 GB/s measured in the memory-hierarchy report. Asking for fewer lines hardly shortens a load, so the useful bandwidth falls with the mask instead of the time. Each minion streams 1 KB blocks, and a mask keeps the same rows of every block. The loads therefore hit memory in a sparse, regular pattern that apparently costs nearly as much as reading every line. The cause is not established here (see Caveats). What matters for kernels is that weights to be skipped should sit in SRAM.

A batch-1 layer that skips zeros

This is the case the brief ranked as most relevant to ML: one output of a ReLU layer, y = W x with a batch of one, where most of x is zero. The layer is 1024 outputs × 4096 inputs in fp32 (16 MB of weights). It is spread over all 32 shires, and each shire keeps its 512 KB share of W in its own L2 scratchpad. Minion j of shire s owns 16 outputs and 256 inputs, in 16 slices of 16. For each slice it loads only the rows of Wᵀ whose x is nonzero (a masked TensorLoad), then runs one single-row TensorFMA. Where all 16 x values of a slice are zero, it skips the slice. Sixteen minions then add their partial rows with a TensorReduce tree that stays inside the shire. Latency runs from a shire barrier to the moment the block's result is in registers, averaged over 2,000 repeats per sparsity level. Each level uses one random x, the same for every variant. Every output was checked exactly against the host. The host supplies a bitmask of which x are nonzero. In a real pipeline the layer that produced x would build it, which takes two vector instructions per 8 values (fltm.ps, then read the mask).

µs at 600 MHz: the time of each 16-output block's slowest minion, mean over the 64 blocks. "Compute only" leaves out the reduction: every minion writes its partial row and the host adds them. The energy rows come from separate runs of the compute-only kernel with the power logger on (mean of two runs), and they drew their own x (45 nonzeros at 99% zeros, against 34 here). Their layers took 7.4 µs dense and 2.7 µs at 99%, from the layer rate, which also includes the shire barrier between layers. "Board" includes the 24.6–24.7 W idle.

Three effects add up. Masked loads cut the bytes, so at 50% zeros the layer drops from 7.5 to 5.7 µs. After that the per-slice fixed costs take over. Each nonempty slice waits for a load (at least 45 cycles) and an FMA, and the curve flattens at 5.4 µs. The big gains come only from skipping whole slices, which random sparsity allows only near the top: for independent zeros 19% of slices are empty at 90% zeros and 85% at 99% (0.9¹⁶ and 0.99¹⁶; the x drawn here had 15% and 87.5%). Energy per layer above idle falls from 70 to 7 µJ, mostly because skipped slices and masked loads do less work in less time. The tensor unit's own gating adds no speed and little energy here: with every row loaded, 90% zeros in x cut the layer's power above idle only from 9.4 to 8.3 W, about 12%.

Against the A100: no one has published a timing for this exact layer on an A100, so the reference lines are bounds. Launching even an empty kernel costs 2.26 µs, plus 1.44 µs for the kernel itself (measured on an A100-SXM4-80GB). cuBLAS half-precision GEMM has a latency floor of about 4.5 µs. Streaming 16.8 MB of weights from L2 at 2.5–4.5 TB/s takes 3.7–6.7 µs. Added to the 2.26 µs launch, or to cuBLAS's 4.5 µs floor, a dense fp32 layer probably takes 6–11 µs, against 7.3–7.5 µs here. CUDA graphs cut launch cost to about 1.3 µs per kernel (measured on H100). At batch 1 a GPU's sparse GEMV is floor-bound too: TEAL's kernel gets 3× at 99–100% sparsity on a 4096×14336 fp16 layer, with 14× this one's weights and 7× its bytes (102 → 33 µs), and Deja Vu reported 1.8–2× end to end at about 75% sparsity (OPT-175B on 8 A100s, against FasterTransformer). So at batch 1 ET-SoC-1 reaches A100-class latency on one layer, and gets below the GPU's per-kernel launch floor only at very high sparsity. That comparison favours ET: its 2.1 µs is a layer inside a kernel that is already running (a launch from the host took 0.2–0.4 ms on this card), and a GPU can avoid launches too, with a persistent kernel whose grid-wide sync costs 1.1–1.2 µs (on-chip communication report). The whole board spends 74–251 µJ per layer, idle included. No A100 energy for this layer has been published; the benchmark in scenario 2 would measure it. The larger prize is a stack of such layers. A pipeline of tens of small layers could stay on chip with no launch between layers, but each layer would still have to pass its outputs across shires (a 1,024-minion allreduce takes 2.3 µs for 32 B and 5.4 µs for 1 KB), and a GPU could run the same stack as one persistent kernel. None of this is measured here.

Divergence: narrow lanes help, but not enough

The second brief proposed a divergence sweep. Each work item needs k iterations of an FMA loop (8 independent chains), and k is drawn from a heavy-tailed Pareto distribution with mean 64. The case for ET-SoC-1 is that its SIMD is only 8 lanes wide and each core runs its own code, so a long item wastes less. All 2,048 harts took 32-item chunks from a shared work queue (a global atomic counter), 524,288 items in all, and every result was checked. Three variants ran. Static runs 8 items per 8 lanes until the longest finishes, as a GPU warp would. Refill gives a lane its next item as soon as it finishes. Scalar runs one item at a time on one lane.

Static 8-lane groups keep busy exactly the share of lanes that grouping the same items in eights predicts: 0.55 at α = 3 and 0.21 at α = 1.2, against 0.35 and 0.08 for a 32-lane warp on the same items. (The continuous order-statistics formula gives 0.55 and 0.19; the cap at 16,384 iterations trims the heaviest tail.) Refilling lanes raises ET's efficiency to 0.96 and 0.46, but its bookkeeping costs cycles: at α = 3 it does fewer useful FMAs per second than static (0.59 against 0.73 T/s), and it pulls ahead only from α = 2. It is not enough to beat a modelled A100. This loop peaks at about 1.5 useful FMAs per cycle per minion, or 0.9 T/s for the chip, under a fifth of the 8-lane peak (4.9 T/s at 600 MHz). An A100 has 9.75 T FP32 FMA/s. A naive kernel that keeps only 8% of its lanes busy at α = 1.2 would still do 0.74 T/s if it issued FMAs at the A100's peak (a model, not a measurement), against ET's measured 0.39 T/s. That 1.9× gap is within what tuning might recover, but at α = 1.5–3 the modelled A100 stays 2.3–4.6× ahead of ET's faster variant, and GPUs have compaction and refill tricks of their own. The loop is limited by instruction issue and by the mask-to-branch round trip; hand-tuning might give 2×, not 10×. Divergence in how long items run is not an ET-SoC-1 advantage. What the chip has that a GPU lacks is one instruction stream per core. That matters when items run different code, as in interpreters, simulators and heterogeneous state machines, which this sweep does not test.

Where ET-SoC-1 should beat an A100, and how to prove it

These measurements and the fact-checked literature give one recipe for a win. The work must be latency-bound or event-driven, so that a GPU sits at its per-kernel or per-sync floor. Its state must fit in the 80 MB of on-chip scratchpad, or be regenerated rather than fetched. And each step's surviving work must be dense enough for 8-lane SIMD or the tensor unit. The chip's sync costs come from the on-chip communication report, measured on the sister card. A 1,024-minion allreduce takes 2.3 µs, a chip-wide barrier from atomics and credits 8.3 µs, and a shire allreduce 0.72 µs. A message round trip takes 113–190 ns inside a shire and 250 ns + 20 ns per mesh hop across shires.

Where the A100 wins

  • Sparse data that streams from DRAM: SpMV, embedding tables, MoE experts. The A100 does FP64 CSR SpMV at 50–245 GFLOP/s, reaching 1.2–1.5 TB/s of effective bandwidth on the best large matrices, while this card streams 76 GB/s. Masked loads do not help once the whole chip streams from DRAM, as measured above.
  • Anything that batches: big GEMMs, batched inference, batched sequence search. So does 2:4 structured sparsity, which doubles A100 tensor-core math (1.5–1.9× measured on large GEMMs). It needs pre-pruned static weights, though, and does nothing for dynamic activation zeros at batch 1.
  • Divergence in run length alone: shown above. Narrow lanes give 1.6–2.8× better lane efficiency, but a modelled A100 kernel at its full 9.75 T FMA/s stays ahead. That peak is 2× ET's 8-lane peak at 600 MHz, and this ET loop reaches under a fifth of its own.

What these measurements change in the starting brief

  • Zero-skip is energy only. The first question the brief asked, "does zero-skip save cycles or only energy?", now has an answer: only energy. In a loop of TensorFMAs that is a lot, about 86% of the op's dynamic power. In the batch-1 layer, where loads dominate, 90% zeros alone cut power above idle by only about 12%. The same holds for tensor_mask on TensorFMA.
  • The fp16 skip condition in the manual is indeed wrong, as the brief suspected. The silicon computes the correct sum.
  • Masked TensorLoads save time from on-chip memory, and from DRAM for a lone minion, but not when the whole chip streams from DRAM. The brief's "inactive neurons cost neither bandwidth nor FMAs" holds for the loads of SRAM-resident weights. A zero still spends its TensorFMA cycles, though, unless its whole 16-element slice is skipped.
  • Sync costs are now measured. A chip-wide barrier made of atomics and credits takes 8.3 µs; the hardware tree does the same in 2.3 µs. The brief's per-step budget of ≤ 10 µs for all-gather plus barrier looks achievable, but only through the tree: a 1,024-minion allreduce takes 2.3 µs for 32 B and 5.4 µs for 1 KB. An all-gather itself was not measured.
  • TensorSend works across shires. The early hang came from a minion changing partners without a barrier: each minion has one "ready" bit for point-to-point transfers, while tree operations keep one bit per level. Any irregular messaging design must respect that.
  • On paper, 1,088 cores × 8 lanes at ~1 GHz is 8.7 T FMA/s. A simple vector-FMA loop here reaches 0.9 T/s at 600 MHz. The tensor unit reaches 4.6 T multiply slots/s (9.5 TFLOP/s dense in the matmul report).
  • Literature corrections found by the fact-check:
    • Deja Vu's end-to-end speedup was at about 75% sparsity, not 85%.
    • Manticore's final paper reports 2.1–4.2× geometric means (by host and Verilator mode), not 5.3×.
    • RTLflow (Lin et al., ICPP 2022) ran on an RTX A6000, not an A100.
    • VAMP's paper median is 40 µs.
    • The A100 PD14 numbers are from Golosio et al. 2023, and no A100 energy per synaptic event has been published.
    • CapelliniSpTRSV's 6.84 GFLOPS and 4.74× are averages over three pre-A100 GPUs in FP64.

How it was measured

  • Kernel and host. workloads/sparsity is a standalone kernel plus host built on the runtime API, as in the other workloads. It was built on the lab machine against its /opt/et, and every probe was validated first in sys_emu with the VPU hazard checker (-vpurf_warn). The checker found two register-file hazards (errata 1.29 types B and C), which the kernel now avoids.
  • Timing is the minion cycle counter (hpmcounter3) on the timed section of each hart. The service processor reported 600 MHz in every sample, logged about 4 times a second during the sweeps, so cycles convert to µs at 1.667 ns.
  • TensorFMA probe. One A tile and one B tile are loaded into the L1 scratchpad once, then 20,000 ops (4,000 on all 1,024 minions) run back to back, accumulating in f0–f31. Zeros are placed by the host: scattered, whole columns, whole rows, or one element of each fp16 pair. Results are compared exactly while fp32 sums stay below 2²⁴, and modulo 2³² for int8.
  • TensorLoad probe. 20,000 16-line loads per minion on one minion (2,000 on all 1,024), two in flight, over private 1 KB blocks. For DRAM the buffer is 64 MB for a lone minion and 256 KB per minion for the whole chip, more than the 32 MB L3 either way. For L2 it is 8 KB per minion, and for the scratchpad 64 KB of the shire's. GB/s are the bytes the mask let through over the slowest minion's cycle count.
  • Layer probe. W is staged into the scratchpads with TensorLoadL2Scp. x is 4,096 small integers, a random fraction of them zero, with a precomputed bitmask. A block's time is its slowest minion's. Each layer starts with a fast-local-barrier plus credit barrier in every shire. The barrier polls its credit counter with a timeout, so a missing minion fails the run rather than hanging the shared card.
  • Divergence probe. k = ceil(Pareto(α)) with mean 64, capped at 16,384. Chunks of 32 items come off a global atomic counter. Lane state lives in f1–f9, the running lanes are the mask m0, and every item's result must equal its k. Lane efficiency is useful lane-iterations over 8 × vector iterations. Throughput is taken over the mean hart time, which is the steady state; the slowest hart is set by the single longest item. The A100 lines apply the warp-of-32 order statistics to the same draws, times 9.75 T FMA/s.
  • Energy. A background thread read board power, clock and voltage from the service processor about 9 times a second. Each configuration then ran 4 s of launches in its own timeout 10 process, with 6 s of idle at the start and end. The second run used the reverse order. The two agree within 0.5 W above idle, except the spin baseline (0.6 W).

Caveats

  • A100 numbers are published measurements on other shapes, plus labelled estimates. None of this was run on an A100. The layer comparison in particular needs the benchmark in scenario 2.
  • One card, one day. aifoundry3 held 600 MHz throughout. Its sister card aifoundry2 moved between 600 and 850 MHz in earlier runs, so on-chip cycle counts carry over but ns and W may not.
  • The zero-skip energy saving is measured on a synthetic loop with A and B in the L1 scratchpad. In a real kernel the loads and data movement add power that sparsity does not remove: in the batch-1 layer, 90% zeros alone saved about 12% of the power above idle.
  • The DRAM result for masked loads (no speedup when the whole chip streams, though a lone minion speeds up) is unexplained. Striding, page policy and the 8-way memory-controller interleave are candidates. A per-memshire sweep would tell.
  • The layer's bitmask of nonzero x comes from the host. The tree reduction assumes that all 16 minions of a block take part in every layer.
  • The divergence loop is not hand-optimized, and PMU events such as TXFMA ops need M-mode to program, so micro-op counts were not read.
  • The scenario estimates in the table are back-of-envelope. Scenario 2 is the only one measured end to end, for one layer.
