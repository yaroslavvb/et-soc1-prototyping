Don’t fix the starvation. Move the line. And don’t build a 2D systolic array.

L2 mainline starvation on the ET-SoC-1, and the systolic path around it

An analysis, written on 22 Sep before the reproduction that afternoon, of a result Ivan reported in the AI Foundry Discord. At a shire’s cache, requests arriving from other shires over the mesh (“L3-slave” requests) outrank the shire’s own (“L2”) requests; the rule is stated in counters-and-dram.md §5. Part of the ET-SoC-1 measurement reports.

The rule affects none of the measurements published before it. The one knob that weights it, l3_yield, is out of reach from user mode and, by the vendor’s own errata, not a reliable fix; the default is probably right anyway. The fix that works, placement, costs 3% of the chip. A systolic design built on TensorSend is immune to all of it, but has a separate hazard: a 2D array hangs a hart until the chip is reset.

Update. Later the same day, 22 Sep, we reproduced this on two cards (One hot line stops a shire). The ~60 M atomics/s aggregate and the even split among the 31 remote shires hold. The reported owner penalty does not. Shire 0 was said to get 6% of an even split, which leaves each other shire 103%, about 17 times as much; measured, the owner’s share of the atomic is 1.004 of an even split, because a global atomic cannot take the local path (through the self ID 0x7F it is a bus error), so the owner’s atomics queue at the same L3-slave port as everyone else’s. What starves is the owner shire’s other memory traffic: its own scratchpad and DRAM loads fall to 0.01–0.05% of normal for as long as the line is hammered. Errata 4.1 (RTLMIN-6207) and 4.2 (RTLMIN-6214) describe the mechanism. The placement advice and the systolic sections below still stand. The owner penalty and the reading of l3_yield as the lever do not; the sections below are corrected.

Terms. A minion is one of the chip’s small RISC-V cores, with two hardware threads (harts); eight minions form a neighbourhood and 32 a shire. Each shire has 4 MB of SRAM, split into an L2 cache, a slice of the chip-wide L3 and a scratchpad (software-managed memory any shire can address). Requests from a shire’s own minions are L2 (neighbourhood) requests; requests arriving from other shires over the mesh are L3-slave requests. amoaddg is a global atomic add, performed at the line’s home shire. FLB is the fast local barrier. TensorSend and TensorRecv move registers from one minion straight into another’s. Kernels run in user mode (U-mode), firmware in machine mode (M-mode). Five questions, answered

QuestionAnswer
What’s the practical impact?Small, if nothing polls a hot line from many shires. It bites when about 24 or more requesters in other shires hammer one line (20 still leave the home shire at 99%): the home shire’s own loads stop. The first relay kernel hit exactly this with 32 shire leaders spinning on one barrier counter, and hung (Hand it to the next shire).
Is it fixable?Not reliably by l3_yield. The errata say it does not rescue a neighbourhood request to the same scratchpad address (RTLMIN-6207) and has no sub-bank granularity (RTLMIN-6214); whether it would help the owner’s other traffic is untested. It is also out of reach: l3_yield_priority is a 5-bit field of sc_reqq_ctl, an M-mode register that no syscall writes. Placement fixes it, and so does pacing the remote shires: at 10,000 cycles between atomics the owner gets back 54% of its memory throughput, at 16,000 about 95% (One hot line stops a shire, §6).
Should it be fixed?No. The default is the right global heuristic, and the 21 queue entries reserved for L3-slave requests look like deadlock avoidance. Changing it risks mesh backpressure to solve a problem placement solves for free.
How would one fix it?Home hot structures in a shire that does no compute. The options are below, cheapest first; the first needs no permissions and no firmware.
And a systolic design?Completely immune: TensorSend and TensorRecv never touch the shire cache. But a 2D array hangs a hart until the chip is reset, for a different reason.

The owner’s atomics were not last; its loads stopped

In the reproduction the owner’s atomics finish alongside everyone else’s. Its ordinary loads are what stop: 384 operations in a window where the mesh retires six million atomics (One hot line stops a shire, §3).

The vendor wrote it down

Errata 4.1 and 4.2 (RTLMIN-6207 and RTLMIN-6214, both Postponed; listed as 3.1 and 3.2 in the errata’s table of contents) describe the shire cache favouring L3-slave requests. They say l3_yield_priority does not rescue a neighbourhood request to the same scratchpad address, and has no sub-bank granularity. The workaround they give is to slow the remote polling.

60 M atomics/s is the aggregate

Per shire it would mean about 1.9 G/s on one line, but a sub-bank accepts one request every 2 cycles: 300 M/s at 600 MHz, about a sixth of that. The reproduction measured 60 M/s in total.

So a global atomic costs ~10 cycles of bank

60 M/s at 600 MHz = 10 cycles each, ~5 sub-bank slots per atomic rather than 1. (Measured directly that afternoon: 10.00 cycles per atomic; One hot line stops a shire, §2.)

When it bites: one question decides it

Is any single line hammered by about 24 or more minions in other shires (fewer than one shire’s worth) while its home shire is also doing work? If not, ignore this entirely.

Uniform working set → no victim

Every L2 miss homes by PA[10:6], so all 32 shires are requester and server at once. Everyone’s local priority loses equally. A latency reshuffle, not a loss.

Hot structure → the owner’s other work stops, invisibly

A barrier counter, lock, queue head or reduction root hammered by about 24 or more concurrent remote requesters (20 still leave the owner at 99%) saturates its home bank; the owner shire’s own scratchpad and DRAM loads drop to 0.01–0.05% of normal and nothing reports an error.

The numbers published before 22 September all sit in the first regime and are uncontended besides: memprobe (Anatomy of a memory access) times one load at a time, and the latency test of nocbench (On-chip communication) runs one pair at a time with a barrier between. Nothing earlier is invalidated; the contended case is measured in One hot line stops a shire.

The fix, cheapest first

  1. Placement — today, no permissionsRun compute on 31 shires and make the 32nd the owner of every hot shared structure. An owner with no local traffic has nothing to starve, and the other 31 get fair service: the hot-line report measured this, every requester getting its fair share to within half a percent (0.998–1.004 of an even split). Cost 3.1% of the chip. Don’t use the master shire; the firmware uses its scratchpad.
  2. Don’t create the hot line at allPer-shire counters plus a tree reduce instead of one global counter. The on-chip measurements already say so: the allreduce tree is 3.7× faster than the global-atomic chip barrier.
  3. Measure the victim — also todaySYSCALL_PMC_SC_SAMPLE (9) accepts any shire, so a U-mode kernel can read the home shire’s bank counters and quantify this without touching firmware.
  4. Pacing — also today, no permissionsIf the hot line must live in a computing shire, make each remote wait at least 10,000 cycles between atomics: the owner gets 54% of its throughput back at 10,000 (the hammering shires keep 96% of theirs) and about 95% at 16,000 (they keep 61%). Exposing l3_yield_priority through a syscall is not a reliable fix per the errata, and is untested for this case.

The behaviour one actually wants is not inverted priority but placement-controlled priority: “this shire owns this structure, its accesses win.” The hardware does not offer that. Step 1 gets the same outcome by making sure the owner is never a competitor.

The energies were re-measured on 23 September on two cards and read lower: 0.67 pJ/B between the minions of a pair, 2.1 around a neighbourhood or shire, 12–18 across the mesh, which fits about 9.3 + 1.7 pJ/B per mean hop (the energy manual, §5).

Free combine-on-receive is the systolic feature. The accumulate happens in the channel, not in the cell. Natural unit: one shire (32 minions). Inside a neighbourhood only the tree’s pairs (0–1, 0–2, 0–4, 2–3, 4–5, 4–6, 6–7) get the 68-cycle path; a chain through all eight needs at least one 114-cycle hop.

The landmine: a 2D systolic array hangs a hart until the chip is reset

“Never let a minion receive readies from two TensorSend partners at once.”
— docs/et-soc1-notes.md in the repository. One partner_ready_peer bit per minion, not one per partner (A trap: one ready flag per minion).

A classic 2D systolic array — every cell receiving from both north and west — is exactly this case. On silicon it hangs the hart: the firmware’s abort cannot recover it, and only a chip reset or a power cycle clears it. The emulator, sys_emu, tracks partners separately and will run such a design happily.

Safe: 1D rings

Each minion only ever sends to its next, so partners never change. Reach for this first.

Safe: 2D in alternating phases

West-pass, barrier, north-pass, barrier. The barrier is 237 cycles — budget that against 68–114 cycles per hop.

Safe: hardware trees

One ready bit per level. 432 cycles for a 32-minion reduce + broadcast.

Hangs: naive 2D mesh dataflow

Two partners per cell. Hangs, needs a reset, and looks exactly like a board fault. On a shared lab card, tell the other users before trying it.
