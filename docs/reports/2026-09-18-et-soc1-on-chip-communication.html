ET-SoC-1 on-chip communication: the mesh and its message passing, measured

Measured 18 Sep 2026 on aifoundry2 in the AI Foundry lab: one ET-SoC-1 PCIe card, 1,024 compute minions in 32 shires, minion clock 600 MHz and mesh clock 400 MHz throughout. GPU values are from published microbenchmarks (sources at the end). Code: workloads/nocbench in the nekko workspace. Part of the ET-SoC-1 measurement reports.

A GPU's SMs can only reach each other through L2. The ET-SoC-1 has several direct paths. Hart 0 of any of its 1,024 minions can send up to a full vector register file (1 KB) straight into another minion's registers with TensorSend/TensorRecv, and the receiver can add, max or min the data into what it already holds. There are hardware reduction and broadcast trees, credit counters that let a core sleep until another core signals it, and barrier counters in every shire. This page measures each of these and maps where the 32 shires sit on the chip's 2D mesh. Inside a shire a message takes 113–190 ns round trip, and each mesh hop adds 20 ns. A tree allreduce over all 1,024 minions takes 2.3 µs, and moving a byte costs 0.8–2.3 pJ inside a shire. Crossing the mesh has a cost, though: there is 7–30× less message bandwidth between shires than inside them.

Latency grows with distance, except through memory

Round-trip time between minion 0 of every pair of shires, against the number of mesh hops between them, for three ways to signal another core. The TensorSend and credit round trips each rise by 20 ns per hop. A flag handoff through global atomics in memory, which is how a GPU passes a signal between SMs, lands on whichever shire's L3 slice holds the flag's line. It costs 600–1,150 ns (median 870) whatever the distance. Across shires the credit and TensorSend round trips almost coincide. Points at 0 hops are pairs inside one shire.

Inside a shire, only the tree edges are fast

Each shire has 32 minions in four neighbourhoods of eight. Measuring all 496 minion pairs in shires 0 and 24 gives exactly two speeds:

  • 68 cycles (113 ns) for 28 pairs per shire: in every neighbourhood, 0–1, 0–2, 0–4, 2–3, 4–5, 4–6 and 6–7.
  • 114–115 cycles (190 ns) for all 468 others. Being in the same neighbourhood doesn't help.

Those seven edges are the reduction tree, and they are the only links of the neighbourhood's fast local messaging network (core-et Neighborhood MAS §4.7), which delivers a 256-bit message in one cycle. Every other message leaves the neighbourhood and comes back through the shire crossbar.

Message size and bandwidth

Time per message for a one-way stream of messages from one minion to another, against message size. The receiver posts a new receive as soon as the last one finishes. A message is up to 127 registers of 32 B, reusing registers once past f31. Each path has a fixed cost per message (40, 86, 135 and 224 cycles) plus a cost per register (2.3, 4.0, 3.0 and 4.6 cycles). Beyond about 5 hops, messages of 1 KB and up slow down: the sender seems to have a limited number of packets in flight.

What the numbers say

  1. This card has marty1885's shire layout. He built the map from shire-to-shire bandwidth on another card. On this card, TensorSend round trips, credit round trips and scratchpad loads all fit a+b×(Manhattan distance on his map), with worst residuals of 1–5 cycles. A search starting from random layouts converged on the same distances every time. Pure Manhattan distance means the routes are shortest paths through all 36 cells, the four non-compute ones included. Minion 31 of each shire gives the same numbers as minion 0, so each shire meets the mesh at a single point.
  2. The mesh costs 20 ns per hop, in both directions together, at any minion clock. That is 12 minion cycles at 600 MHz, or 4 mesh cycles each way at 400 MHz. The memory-hierarchy runs, where the governor moved the clock mid-row, also show it: remote scratchpad loads cost 66 minion cycles + (56 ns + 20 ns × hops), and fit within a cycle at 600, 700 and 800 MHz.
  3. Three speeds, not four. A message takes 68 cycles on a reduction-tree edge, 114 anywhere else in the shire, and 150 + 12 per hop between shires. A neighbourhood boundary costs nothing extra; only the tree edges are special. Chains and trees should put partners on those edges.
  4. Combining in the channel is free. A receive that adds or maxes into its registers takes the same time as a plain copy. That is the ⊕ of a (min,+) or (max,+) semiring, applied on arrival without a separate instruction.
  5. The hardware trees are the fastest way to synchronise the whole chip. A 1,024-minion allreduce of 32 B takes 2.3 µs, against 8.3 µs for a barrier made from global atomics and credits. Inside a shire it takes 0.72 µs, and all 32 shires can reduce at once without slowing down.
  6. The shire boundary is a bandwidth cliff. Messages move 1.1–3.0 TB/s in aggregate inside shires and 0.09–0.16 TB/s between them, at 6–20× the energy per byte. Across shires, bulk data moves better as TensorLoads from the other shire's scratchpad (0.98 TB/s at 6.3 pJ/B) than as messages. That helps explain marty1885's finding that systolic matmul and FlashAttention ran no faster than sharing data inside each shire: the L2, L3 and scratchpad paths carry the bulk traffic.
  7. Latency in nanoseconds is GPU-like; the mechanisms and the energy are not. A message crossing 1–10 hops takes 135–225 ns one way. An A100 takes 166 ns to hand a flag from one SM to another through L2. The differences lie elsewhere. The data lands in the receiver's registers, with an optional combine. There are 1,024 addressable endpoints instead of 108 SMs, plus hardware trees and sleeping credit waits. A byte costs 0.8–20 pJ to move, against about 38 pJ for one A100 L2 access.

A trap: one ready flag per minion

Before a sender may transmit, TensorSend waits for a "ready" message from its receiver. In the RTL (dcache_reduce.v, register partner_ready_peer), a point-to-point ready is a single bit per minion, not one per partner. If two different minions post their readies to the same sender before it has used them, one of them is lost. That receiver then waits forever, and so does its sender. The tree operations are safe: they keep one bit per tree level. The first cross-shire test here ran two pairs through minion 0.0 without a barrier between them, and it hung the card. The stalled harts ignored the firmware's abort, and every later launch failed with KernelLaunchCmIfaceMulticastFailed until the chip was reset.

The simulator (sys_emu) tracks every partner separately, so it never shows the problem. nocbench now refuses any schedule where a minion could have two partners' readies outstanding. Pairs are separated by a chip-wide barrier, and rings only ever receive readies from the next minion. With that rule, TensorSend works across all 496 shire pairs. The lab admin asks that a hung card be power-cycled by him rather than reset by users.

What it means for systolic and wavefront designs

  • 1D chains at minion grain inside a shire, 2D arrays at shire grain over the mesh. A one-way message costs about 34 cycles on a tree edge, 57 cycles elsewhere in the shire, and 75 + 6 cycles per hop between shires. To keep communication under a tenth of the time, a cell needs about 10× that much work per step: roughly 350–600 cycles inside a shire and 750–1,000 at a shire boundary. That sets the grain k ≈ tmsg/tcell for wavefront DP or lattice updates.
  • Order chains by the map, not by shire ID. Stepping through shires 0, 1, 2, … averages 3.3 hops per boundary and up to 8. This ring through all 32 compute shires moves one hop at every step: 0 24 9 25 2 11 19 27 18 10 17 14 22 26 15 23 31 7 6 30 29 5 28 20 12 21 13 1 16 4 3 8, and back to 0.
  • Send boundaries, load bulk. Registers-to-registers messages suit small, latency-critical exchanges and in-network combines. Tiles that move between shires go faster through the scratchpads and L3.
  • Reductions and barriers belong to the trees. They beat atomics by 3.7× chip-wide, run in parallel in every shire, and their per-level cost follows the distance of each level's partners.

How it was measured

  • Round trips. Hart 0 of two minions bounces COUNT registers back and forth with TensorSend and TensorRecv (or credits, or atomic flags) 300–4,000 times after a warm-up, timed with the minion cycle counter. Pairs run one at a time, with a chip-wide barrier between them. The receiver checks the data: after a MOVE both sides must hold the sender's pattern, and after ADD or MAX the value a host-side replay predicts. All 3,699 pair measurements and all 44 stream, ring, tree and barrier runs passed. For the shire matrix, minion 0 of each of the 32 shires met every other shire's minion 0 (496 pairs), and the same again with minion 31. The intra-shire matrix covers all 496 minion pairs of shires 0 and 24.
  • Streams, rings, trees and barriers. A stream sends N messages one way. A ring has every minion send to the next and receive from the previous, with neighbours alternating which comes first. Allreduces sum each minion's ID + 1 once with integer ADD, check the result, then time 500–1,000 more with integer MAX, which keeps the sum, and check it again. Barriers are timed over thousands of iterations.
  • Clock. A background loop read the minion clock and board power from the service processor about 4 times a second during every run. The clock stayed at 600 MHz, and nanoseconds are cycles ÷ 0.6.
  • Energy. Board power sampled about 12 times a second, averaged over about 4 s of launches per configuration. The baseline is the median idle power in the 5 s gaps just before and after that configuration, because the card was cooling from 80 °C (another user's matmul) and its idle power drifted from 35.1 to 33.7 W. Two runs, in opposite orders.
  • Lab etiquette. Every card run was its own process under timeout 10, and a script waited for the card to be idle before each one. The one hang is described above. The chip was reset with the user's approval, and a health check passed before any further runs.

Caveats

  • One card, one clock. The logical-to-physical map can differ between cards when a different shire is fused off, and it matched marty1885's card here. All runs were at 600 MHz. Mesh time is fixed in nanoseconds, so at 800 MHz the same hops cost 16 cycles instead of 12.
  • Energy is whole-card power above idle. It includes the stalled or busy cores that issue the messages, and the regulators, so it overstates the cost of the wires. The deltas are small (1.6–2.5 W on a 34 W card), and the per-hop slope's r² is 0.90, so treat the mesh numbers as ±20%.
  • The GPU column is not our measurement. The numbers come from several published microbenchmark studies with their own methods and clocks. No measured A100 small-vector device-wide reduction was found, so the comparison uses its grid.sync().
  • Hart 0 only. Only hart 0 of a minion may issue tensor operations, so hart 1 was idle in these tests. Credits can also target hart 1, which was not measured.
