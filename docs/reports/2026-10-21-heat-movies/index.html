You can see where a chip is thinking from the back of its circuit board

← AI Foundry lab dashboard › Worklog

A thermal camera sits behind an ET-SoC-1 accelerator card. Move 128 of its 1,024 hardware threads from one corner of the die to another every 25 seconds, and the warm patch on the board walks with them.

Thermal Master P3 behind the card; two sessions, 390 heater launches, 71.1 minutes of card time, none failed. Every movie is a difference image, never a raw frame: the swaps against their opposite half, the orbit and the sweep against their lap’s own average, the bloom against the idle before each load step. The panel burnt into each frame says harts, the chip’s own word for hardware threads.

Move the work round the four corners and the heat walks with it

The camera sees the die rolled about a right angle, not mirrored: west→east runs up the picture, north→south runs right. So the corners read NW lower left, NE upper left, SE upper right, SW lower right — and the warm patch walks them clockwise, in all five laps, before collapsing into the middle.

Two clauses of the hero’s own pass rule failed, and that belongs here. The five positions’ board power spread 0.620 W where the rule allowed 0.3, and the centre drew the most — so the centre reading is exploratory; its electrical correction is 23–48 mK against centre-to-corner dipoles of 217–381 mK. And each phase’s frame average reached 86 mK where the rule allowed 25 — but it flips sign when the shutter cleaning changes while the moving lobe does not, so it is the camera’s shutter, not the card’s regulators.

The same pipeline with nothing to find, and it found nothing

A permutation p is no use here: four laps give sixteen ways to flip the labels, so the registered within-lap null cannot return below 0.125 however large the signal. The controls answer instead by size.

The matched-filter position advances in the same order in the averaged run — one chance in 120 — and, on the cleaned frames, in every one of the five laps; without the cleaning, in three of five. The card’s total draw spreads 0.391 W across the five, and on the perpendicular axis all five sit within ±21 mK. Positions post hoc.

Turn the whole chip on and the whole board glows — this is what every other movie cancels

Switch every shire on — 768 threads, about +39 W measured — and the frame mean rises +7.1 K in 42 s, half of it within 14.8 s. That is a change in total power, the one thing the swaps are built to cancel: their whole-frame means sit between −79 and +94 mK, this one at +7,100 mK.

Honestly: the governor stepped the clock in every on-phase and the die reached 69 °C, because the registered 64 °C gate was keyed to 1,024 threads and the amendment ran 768. The clean power law would have drawn 49 W here, and the governor is why it did not. Nothing was unsafe, but the arrival time is an upper bound, not the registered measurement.

Rust bars are the three shapes that run the same 128 threads in different places on the die.

Three shapes of 128 threads draw 0.51 W apart — a hair outside the half-watt the plan allowed, and not proof of anything either way: the legs ran in ascending order and carry 0.8–1.1 W of warm-up drift. Corrected for that drift they agree to 0.37 W and the order reverses. The ladder fits 5.80 ± 0.30 W plus 55.8 ± 1.0 mW a thread, not 40.6 mW from zero. Those six watts of waking up never move with the work: they brighten every picture and shift none.

The board blurs heat over 3–5 cm, so there is no shape and no scale bar

Copper spreads heat 3–5 cm before it reaches the camera — wider than the die itself. A single shire arrives as the same blob as any other pattern of the same power, a checkerboard arrives as nothing, and a better camera buys none of it back: the limit is the board.

The die’s footprint also fills the frame, so the pre-registered gate for a distance scale got no input. Directions only: no distances, no scale bar.

Method, and what changed from the plan

  • Pre-registered — masks, orders, predictions and falsifiers frozen at 16:58 PDT, seven minutes before the first session.
  • Amendment A1, 17:14 PDT, before anything was reduced: the ladder measured about 2.5× the designed power a thread, so 12-shire sets ran at 16 threads a shire and 8-shire sets at 24 — 192 either way — and a 17 s west–east swap was dropped.
  • Post hoc, labelled throughout: per-pixel shutter-step removal, and contrasts read along the other session’s templates. Every pre-registered conclusion survives without them except the replicate — uncleaned its dipole points −21.9°, about 112° away, so both its size and its direction are the cleaning’s doing — and the sweep’s lap-by-lap ordering, which falls from 5 of 5 laps to 3 of 5.
  • The camera was left as the public stream has it. Its shutter fired every 8–10 s under load, so the registered exclusion window could not be applied. The same rule says a block that would lose more than 30% of its frames is reported as a bound, not a value — and the window would have removed 45–82% of every block, so under the rule as written every amplitude here is a bound. Where the rule could be approximated the answer does not move.
  • Registered p floors: four laps cannot go below 0.125, two laps below 0.5; the five-arm blocks are not so limited. And one bug: the bloom’s 64 °C die gate was guarded on a thread count A1 had changed, so it never read the die.
  • Also off the plan: the amendment’s power branch fired on an extrapolation rather than the registered rung; the hero’s session ran before the gain map existed, so it was analysed without one; the orientation fit the protocol registers for the hero was excluded by the pipeline and run by hand; the registered clock fallback was never coded; the instrumental null’s second, two-lap reduction was never produced; and the movies render 0.5 s bins at 24 fps with a 2×2 spatial bin against a frozen table of 1 s bins at 8–10 fps and 4×4 — finer, uniform across the set, and burnt into every frame.
  • The runs: 17:05:59–17:24:52, then until 18:17:12. Code · data · the previous experiment.
