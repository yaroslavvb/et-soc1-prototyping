The ET-SoC-1 memory hierarchy, measured on silicon, next to the A100

Measured 18 Sep 2026 on aifoundry2 in the AI Foundry lab: one ET-SoC-1 PCIe card, 32 GB LPDDR4X, 1,024 compute minions. A100 values are from published microbenchmarks and energy studies (sources at the end). Code: workloads/memhier in the nekko workspace.

Pointer-chase and streaming probes give the card's memory hierarchy as a spec sheet. ET-SoC-1's on-chip memories are fast and cheap per byte: a load hits L1 in 8.8 ns and the shire's L2 in 78 ns, against 23 ns and 148 ns on an A100, at 7–9× less energy per byte. They are also tiny per core: the firmware leaves each hart only 512 B of L1 data cache. Off chip the picture flips. DRAM latency is about equal to the A100's HBM (~440 vs 405 ns), but the card streams 76 GB/s, about a twentieth of an A100, at roughly 1.4× the energy per byte.

Scratchpad latency across the mesh

Any minion can load from any shire's 2.5 MB scratchpad. Each row is one requesting shire; each cell is the latency to one of the 32 shires' scratchpads (a 64 KB chain, so every load misses L1). Darker is slower. The diagonal cells are the local scratchpad at 47 cycles; remote ones cost 112–271 cycles, in steps of 12 or 16 cycles, which look like mesh hops.

What the numbers say

  1. Kernels get only 512 B of L1 per hart. Before every launch the firmware puts the L1 in scratchpad mode (PRM table 8.4), turning 3 KB of the 4 KB into the tensor unit's scratchpad. The chase's first knee sits exactly at 512 B. A user-mode kernel can switch to split mode, where hart 0 gets 2 KB, but the switch invalidates L1 lines without writing them back, so it was not tried on a shared card.
  2. There is a 2 KB level between L1 and L2: the L2's read buffer, an 8-entry fully associative cache per bank for clean lines. Working sets up to 2 KB hit it at 36 cycles, 11 fewer than the L2 array. That is the same 11-cycle difference the shire cache spec gives (10 vs 21 shire cycles).
  3. The L2 scratchpad is the L2 without the tags. It has the same 47-cycle latency and even goes through the read buffer and L1, but reading it at the same bandwidth costs 2.8 pJ/B instead of 4.3.
  4. Distance on the mesh is visible. A remote scratchpad load costs 186–452 ns depending on the pair of shires, in 12–16-cycle steps. Latency is also asymmetric: shire 0 → 31 is 220 cycles, 31 → 0 is 271. The L3 hides this by spreading lines over every shire, so all L3 hits pay an average trip of about 280 ns.
  5. The card changes its clock under load. It runs a "managed power" governor with a 65 W target. During the runs the telemetry showed the minion clock moving between 600 and 700 MHz (518–595 mV), and wall times imply up to about 850 MHz. On-chip latencies are fixed in cycles; L3 and DRAM, which cross into the mesh's 400 MHz and the DRAM's 933 MHz domains, are fixed in nanoseconds instead. That is why DRAM measured 287–368 cycles but a steadier 421–486 ns.
  6. Most of the energy is being awake, not moving bytes. Any kernel on all 1,024 minions adds 6–13 W over the 27 W idle, whatever it reads. So energy per byte is lowest where bandwidth is highest: 1.8 pJ/B from L1, 148 pJ/B from DRAM. Against a spin loop that touches no data, L1 reads add only 0.14 pJ/B.

How it was measured

  • Latency. One hart chases a random cyclic chain with one pointer per 64 B line, which no prefetcher can predict. After warm-up (two passes, capped at 219 loads), 40,000 dependent loads are timed with the minion cycle counter. Each chase's final pointer is checked against a host-side walk of the same chain, and every one of the ~230 chases passed. Chains up to 256 MB live in DRAM. Scratchpad chains are written into the target shire's scratchpad by the chasing hart.
  • Bandwidth. Hart 0 of all 1,024 minions streams 1 KB TensorLoads (which bypass L1 but use L2), two in flight, over private slices sized to hit L2 (8 KB per minion, 256 KB per shire), L3 (24 MB in total) or DRAM (256 MB), or over the local or a remote scratchpad. L1 bandwidth uses 32 B vector loads by both harts of every minion over a private 256 B buffer. Throughput is bytes over wall time.
  • Energy. Board power from the card's service processor, sampled about 7 times a second, averaged over each configuration's launch windows (about 4 s each) and compared with idle. The numbers are the mean of two complete runs, which agreed within 0–20% depending on the level (DRAM varied most).
  • Lab etiquette. Every card run was its own process under timeout 10, and the card was checked for other users before each. One energy run crashed the host runtime during the DRAM stream, and the card's master firmware then refused kernel launches. A full chip reset through the management interface restored it, and the upstream hello-world test and these probes were re-verified afterwards.

Caveats

  • The A100 column is not our measurement. Latency and bandwidth come from several published microbenchmark studies, which agree within a few percent. The energy numbers come from a single study (SC'25). Their method is close to ours but not identical.
  • ET energy per byte is whole-card power above idle. It includes the busy chip's floor, DRAM chips, regulators and PCIe, so it overstates the cost of the memory arrays themselves, most of all for DRAM.
  • The clock was not pinned. The card's own governor was left in charge (it is a shared machine), so bandwidth and power are averages over whatever clock it chose. Some per-hart cycle counters in the two-hart tests also read high, probably erratum 1.23 (simultaneous PMU reads by both harts), so bandwidth uses wall time only.
  • Peak-rate microbenchmarks. Real kernels mix levels, and bank conflicts, the mesh, and the DRAM controller's page policy will change the picture.

Reproduce

# on a Mac with the nekko workspace, build on the lab machine
scripts/deploy-lab.sh aifoundry2 workloads/memhier

# latency (each command holds the card for a few seconds)
ssh aifoundry2 'cd ~/nekko/build/memhier && timeout 10 host/memhier_host --test chase'
ssh aifoundry2 'cd ~/nekko/build/memhier && timeout 10 host/memhier_host --test chase --where scp --scp-shire all --sizes 64K'

# bandwidth and energy (quit et-powertop first; every configuration runs under timeout 10)
ssh aifoundry2 'cd ~/nekko && python3 workloads/memhier/run_energy.py --host-bin build/memhier/host/memhier_host --out build/memhier-energy'

# numbers and chart data for this page
python3 workloads/memhier/analyze.py docs/reports/data/2026-09-18-memhier-aifoundry2 --embed docs/reports/2026-09-18-et-soc1-memory-hierarchy.html
