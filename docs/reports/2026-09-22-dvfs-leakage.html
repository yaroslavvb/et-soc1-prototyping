The ET-SoC-1's DVFS loop, and the leakage it never switches off

AI Foundry's three ET-SoC-1 machines, 21–22 September 2026 · firmware 353f20e, open RTL b38a1a3 · checking six claims from a conversation with David Kanter (MLPerf / MLCommons) against the source, the RTL and the silicon · part of the measurement reports

David Kanter described how a mature chip manages its own power: a DVFS loop that knows , computes its own power estimate from activity counters because voltage and frequency are already known, folds in thermal sensors because leakage depends on temperature, and puts cache data arrays behind leakage-suppression transistors so that only the ~10% of an array in use is awake. He added a hedge: maybe not on Esperanto's part, but on anything from a more mature company. The hedge was right. This chip's loop measures power with a sensor instead of estimating it, runs with no hysteresis and therefore hunts, and its per-minion sleep transistors are real but tied off. What is left is leakage: 36% of a busy card and 64% of an idle one, against the 5–30% he called typical.

1. The loop as built

The whole governor is one function, check_power_throttle_conditions() in ServiceProcessorBL2/services/thermal_pwr_mgmt.c, called once per pass of the device-management task. Reduced to its logic:

every DM_TASK_DELAY_MS = 10 ms:
    T   = pvt_get_minion_avg_temperature()          // on-die thermal sensor
    P   = pmic_read_average_soc_power()             // MEASURED board power, from the PMIC over I2C
    if  active_power_management and master minion is BUSY:
        if   T > 65 °C       and f > f_min:  step DOWN one VMIN-LUT point
        elif P > 65 W        and f > f_min:  step DOWN one VMIN-LUT point
        elif P < 65 W        and f < f_max:  step UP   one VMIN-LUT point
    on master minion going IDLE:                     // between kernels
        set frequency back to the boot point

Four things follow, and all four show up in the measurements below.

  • The input is a measurement, not an estimate. pmic_read_average_soc_power() is a read of the power-management IC's own averaged wattage. Nothing in the loop counts bus bits, instructions or activity factors. Kanter's is not evaluated anywhere in this firmware, because the chip does not need to infer the left-hand side — it has a meter.
  • Thermal has priority. The temperature test comes first, and it is a plain if, so when the die is above 65 °C the power branch is never reached. A card that has been busy idles above 65 °C and is therefore pinned at its lowest operating point regardless of how little power it draws.
  • There is no hysteresis. The file defines UPPER_POWER_THRESHOLD_GUARDBAND and LOWER_POWER_THRESHOLD_GUARDBAND, a ±5% band around the TDP level — and then never uses them. Both macros are dead code at this commit. The loop compares against the raw threshold in both directions.
  • The clock resets at every kernel boundary. When the master minion reports idle the service processor calls go_to_idle_state_and_update_pwr_status(), which sets the boot frequency. A stream of short kernels therefore never holds a raised clock.

2. The loop as measured

The governor only moves when the die starts below 65 °C, which on this card means after a night of idling. Seven such runs exist, from the cool-start session of 21 September, sampled at 10 Hz. They contain 36 clock transitions. Voltage moved with frequency in every one of them: 600 MHz at 0.517 V, 700 at 0.568, 800 at 0.618. This is real DVFS, not frequency scaling alone.

One run of constant data from a 64 °C die: the governor hunts. Blue: minion clock. Orange: board power.

It is a limit cycle, and the dead guardband explains it. The loop sees an averaged power number that lags by about two seconds, has no hysteresis band, and steps one operating point at a time. So it climbs until it trips a threshold, drops, sees the threshold clear, climbs again — with a period of roughly two seconds, which is the lag. Over a 7-second run of constant data the clock changed seven times and the workload spent most of it at neither the top nor the bottom point.

Every down-step is accounted for, and none of them is the power branch acting alone. Of the 18: seven were thermal only (the die above 65 °C while the board drew 45–56 W, well under the limit), four were both at once (random data at 800 MHz draws up to 88 W and heats fast), and seven were not throttling at all — they are the boot-frequency reset, landing within 12 to 186 ms of a kernel boundary at 64–65 °C and 33–39 W. The power branch never fired on its own, because on this card the only way to exceed 65 W is to be at 800 MHz, and by the time you get there the die is already through 65 °C.

The loop is also slow to start: the first clock change came 0.39 to 0.99 s after the kernel launched, never in milliseconds, even though the task itself runs every 10 ms. Once moving, it stepped at intervals at or below our 100 ms sampling period.

3. The leakage-suppression transistors

Kanter's description was specific: cache data arrays sit behind leakage-suppression transistors, a lookup checks the tags first and un-suppresses only the part of the array it needs, and the cost is a small wake-up latency. The same trick at the granularity of whole cores and execution units.

The hardware is there. The open RTL of the neighbourhood carries the full interface: a per-minion sleep control and its acknowledge (pwr_ctrl_min_nsleepin / nsleepout, one bit per minion), a global pair, and per-minion isolation (pwr_ctrl_min_isolate) with level shifters and a power stub that forces the outputs safe while a domain is down. That is a textbook power-gating structure, sized for the granularity he described.

Nothing drives it. In the open configuration the whole interface is tied off, with a comment saying so:

// Power Ctrl - power control handled outside cpu subsystem and this ifce is not used
.pwr_ctrl_glb_nsleepin ('1),   // '1 = never asleep
.pwr_ctrl_min_nsleepin ('1),
.pwr_ctrl_min_isolate  ('0),

And the service-processor firmware, searched at the card's own commit, contains no power-gating control of any kind: the only PWR_CTRL hits in the whole tree are an eMMC controller's bus voltage. Whatever drives those pins on the taped-out part, it is not this firmware, and the chip-level netlist is not in the open drop.

And the silicon shows no sign of it. If an array were suppressed once idle, the first access afterwards would pay the wake-up. The probe: put one line at a chosen level, let the minion spin on its cycle counter for a swept interval from 1.7 µs to 27 ms without touching that line, then time a single load of it — same line, same address, only the idle time varying, 20 repeats.

  • L1 and L3: zero. The paired difference between the longest and shortest idle is 0 cycles.
  • L2: −11 cycles, i.e. very slightly faster after idling — noise, not a wake-up.
  • DRAM: +10 cycles, and that one has a known cause. It is the row closing, worth about 40 cycles when it happens, which this experiment's earlier measurements already characterised.

So on this card, in this firmware, the leakage-suppression transistors are a capability that ships switched off. The only gating actually working is clock gating, which the same RTL does use aggressively — per lane, per functional unit, with a seven-cycle tail — and which is why an idle-but-powered core costs so little dynamic power and still leaks.

4. What that costs

Leakage as a share of board power, from the fitted model, against Kanter's typical range

A free out-of-sample check. The leakage curve in this chart, , was fitted on 21 September. This brief opened by sampling the card cold on 22 September, after it had been idle for 20.4 hours in a warmer room: 31.79 ± 0.04 W at 73.0 °C. The curve predicts 31.78 W. A different day, a different ambient, a temperature the fit had barely seen — and the error is 0.01 W.

Of those 31.8 idle watts, 11.1 W sits on the minion rail alone: a thousand cores doing nothing for twenty hours. 2.0 W is the SRAM rail, 3.6 W the mesh, and 15.1 W is on no rail sensor at all (DDR, PCIe, regulator conversion).

Kanter put leakage at 5–30% of a design's power, with 20% common, and noted that parts which clock lower and use slower transistors leak less. This card is outside that range in both directions of use: 36% of a 63 W random-data matmul, 64% of an idle card at 80 °C. Three things make it so, and only one is the chip's fault — it runs at 0.52 V rather than the 0.4 V Esperanto designed for, in a desktop chassis that idles it at 73–80 °C rather than in server airflow, with 160 MB of on-die SRAM and no array power gating in play.

5. What this means for the equation, and for measuring power

  • The equation is right; it is just not what the loop uses. Its activity term is strongly confirmed on this card: power over idle is linear in active minions, 25.6 mW per minion at 256, 512 and 768 active and 27.0 at 1,024, and a model that counts four classes of switching event inside the multiply-add unit predicts board power across fourteen operand patterns to 0.5 W out of sample. The term is only weakly testable here, because the governor will not hold the high operating point long enough on a heavy workload: for constant data it predicts for the switching-power ratio between the two points, and the measurement is consistent but noisy. A clean test needs a die below 65 °C and a runner that pins the work rate.
  • Kanter's "measurement is expensive" argument does not apply to this card. His reason MLPerf cannot mandate measured power is that most submitters will not pay the time and energy to instrument a system. This chip is already instrumented, as a load-bearing part of its own safety loop: a PMIC that averages board power, a service processor that reads it every 10 ms, and a management interface that hands the number to the host. The cost of reporting measured power here is one query.
  • But the loop's hunting is exactly the kind of thing a provisioned-power metric cannot see. A normalisation by provisioned kilowatts scores a card by its nameplate. On this card a badly damped governor moves real throughput by tens of percent within a single 7-second run, invisibly.
  • The one-line fix is already written in the source. Two guardband macros, defined and unused. Using them would give the loop the ±5% dead band it was evidently designed to have.

6. The same firmware on three cards

Everything above is one card. AI Foundry has three machines, and the loop's behaviour turned out to depend on a single byte that differs between them.

aifoundry1 could not be measured. It holds two ET-SoC-1 cards, both enumerated on the PCIe bus, with their device nodes present. Its kernel module is built from different sources than the other two machines (srcversion 1383B256EB24A0A53F04CC7 against 47D26A305A0428B29FB7FC4) while carrying the same libDM.so, and the library's compatibility check refuses to open the card: Error unable to evaluate compatibility!. Its management service is not running. Reinstalling a kernel driver on a shared lab machine is not ours to do, so those two cards are reported here as unavailable and the lab admin has what they need to fix it.

aifoundry2 and aifoundry3 look identical and are not. Same firmware release, same PMIC firmware, same static device configuration over the driver's ioctl: 65 W TDP, 600 MHz boot clock, 32 compute shires, 32 MB of L3. But asked the same question over the management interface, the two service processors give different answers.

The governor compares measured SoC power against that TDP level. With the level at zero, the comparison in check_power_throttle_conditions() can only come out one way:

So on aifoundry3 the loop is not disabled. It runs every 10 ms, and every time it runs it throttles down. The card can reach its lowest operating point and nothing can ever lift it off. The service processor's own log, read back out of the card, says so in as many words:

Power throttle down event, current pwr 35380  tdp level: 0
Power idle state event, current pwr 26100  tdp level 0
Power throttle down event, current pwr 40200  tdp level: 0
Power throttle down event, current pwr 48470  tdp level: 0

A second, independent reading agrees. get_power_state() classifies the card as MAX_POWER whenever power exceeds the TDP level and MANAGED_POWER when it is merely above the 30 W safe threshold. aifoundry3 reports max_power while sitting at 23 W and 600 MHz, which is only consistent with a threshold of zero; aifoundry2, drawing more, reports managed_power. Two different code paths, one cause.

The host cannot see this. The driver's ETSOC1_IOCTL_GET_DEVICE_CONFIGURATION reports a 65 W TDP on all three machines, including aifoundry3. Anything that checks a card's configuration from the host side gets the nameplate number, not the one the governor is using. The card works. It is simply capped at 600 MHz for its whole life, which on a workload that would otherwise reach 800 MHz at 0.618 V is about a third of the throughput, and nothing reports an error.

The contrast is not that aifoundry2 is always faster. A card that has been busy idles above 65 °C, and the thermal branch comes first, so aifoundry2 also sits at 600 MHz most of the time — after thirteen minutes of idling on 22 September it was still at 73 °C and still at 600 MHz. The difference is the reason and the remedy. aifoundry2 reaches 700 and 800 MHz when it starts cool, which is what section 2's 36 transitions are. aifoundry3 cannot, at any temperature, because the branch that would raise it is unreachable.

This also answers the question section 5 left open. The power branch of the loop never fired on its own in any aifoundry2 run, because a card that has been busy idles above 65 °C and the thermal test comes first. On aifoundry3 the power branch is the only branch that fires, continuously, on a die 15 °C below the thermal threshold. It behaves exactly as written.

7. What the second card says about the model

A card pinned at its lowest operating point is still a card, and running the same strict protocol on it is the first real out-of-sample test of the flip-counting model: different silicon, different heatsink, a launch temperature 25 °C lower, and a model whose coefficients were fitted entirely on aifoundry2.

Switching power over idle, eight operand patterns, two cards

The operating point does not explain the gap. aifoundry3 runs its minions at 523 mV against aifoundry2's 518 mV, at the same 600 MHz. By that predicts about 2% more switching power on aifoundry3, not 8% less. Whatever the 8% is — silicon, package, the board's regulator efficiency at that load — it is a property of the card, and one number captures it.

Leakage transfers too, and further than it should. The idle law fitted on aifoundry2 between 62 and 90 °C, extrapolated down to 50–57 °C on the other card, predicts its idle power to within a watt:

The consequence for anyone with a shelf of these cards: the shape of the data-dependence is a property of the design and moves between cards unchanged, while the absolute scale and the idle offset are per-card constants worth about 8% and a watt. One random-data matmul on a new card calibrates both.

8. Method, and what is not established

  • Firmware claims are read from external/et-platform at 353f20e, the commit matching the card's firmware: services/thermal_pwr_mgmt.c, rtos_task/dm_task.c, include/thermal_pwr_mgmt.h, include/bl2_pmic_controller.h, config/mgmt_build_config.h.
  • RTL claims are read from external/core-et at b38a1a3: rtl/shire/neigh/neigh_top.v, neigh_top_pwrstub.v, rtl/cpu_subsystem/cpu_subsystem_top.v. This is the Erbium branch, a later MCU-class configuration of the same core lineage, not the taped-out ET-SoC-1 chip top — which is why "nothing drives the sleep pins" is a statement about the open drop and this firmware, corroborated by the card, not a statement about the silicon's chip-level netlist.
  • The 36 transitions come from the cool-start runs of 21 September, in docs/reports/data/2026-09-21-horace-aifoundry2/cold1 and cold2. The classification into thermal, power and kernel-boundary is mechanical, in tools/ettelem/analyze_dvfs.py; nothing is fitted. Our 10 Hz sampling and the sensor's whole-degree steps mean a transition recorded at 66 °C may have been decided at 65 °C, so the attribution is sound but the exact crossing instants are not.
  • The wake-up probe is gen_ops.py wakeup in workloads/memprobe, one hart, 20 repeats, raw results in docs/reports/data/2026-09-22-dvfs-aifoundry2/wakeup/. It can only detect a wake-up longer than about ten cycles, and only for arrays idled up to 27 ms, which is the widest the delay op encodes. A gating policy that waits longer than that would not show up.
  • The three-machine comparison uses two new read-only tools. tools/etcfg issues ETSOC1_IOCTL_GET_DEVICE_CONFIGURATION to the driver; ettelem config asks the service processor for DM_CMD_GET_MODULE_STATIC_TDP_LEVEL, ..._TEMPERATURE_THRESHOLDS and ..._POWER_STATE. Neither writes anything. The firmware log excerpts are the card's own trace buffer, read with ettelem sptrace and stored verbatim in docs/reports/data/2026-09-22-cards/.
  • aifoundry3's session is the same strict protocol as aifoundry2's, targeted at 55 °C instead of 80 °C because that card idles cooler: 20 recorded runs over eight operand patterns, launched at 55.77 ± 0.18 °C. Raw data in docs/reports/data/2026-09-22-horace-aifoundry3/. The model coefficients applied to it are the aifoundry2 fit, unchanged; the only number derived from aifoundry3 is the single scale factor, and the leave-one-out figure shows what it costs to calibrate that on one run.
  • Not established: whether the taped-out ET-SoC-1 drives its sleep transistors under some other firmware or in some other power state; what the boot frequency is set to in flash; why aifoundry3's flashed TDP is zero, and whether it was ever different; and whether the 8% switching-power gap between the two cards is silicon, package or board regulator, which would need a third working card to separate. The power branch of the loop, left open in the first version of this brief, is now established: it fires exactly as written, on aifoundry3, continuously.
