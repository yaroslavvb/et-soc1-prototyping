The ET-SoC-1's DVFS loop, and the leakage it never switches off

21–22 September 2026 · aifoundry2 and aifoundry3 (aifoundry1 unavailable) · firmware source read at et-platform 353f20e (the cards' own log strings show an older build; see §8), open RTL core-et b38a1a3 (Erbium branch) · checks six claims from a conversation with David Kanter (MLCommons) on 20 September 2026, in private notes · part of the ET-SoC-1 measurement reports

The ET-SoC-1 manages its power more simply than a mature chip, and leakage is where it pays. Its governor reads a power meter instead of estimating power, and its 65 °C threshold has no dead band, so near it the clock hunts. Its open RTL has per-minion sleep controls that are tied off, and the card shows no sign of array power gating. What is left is leakage: 36% of a busy card and 65% of an idle one at 80 °C, above the 5–30% David Kanter called typical. And a second card's firmware holds a power limit of 0 W, which pins it at 600 MHz while the driver still reports 65 W.

The yardstick is how Kanter described a mature chip managing its own power. It runs a DVFS loop (dynamic voltage and frequency scaling) that knows \(P = C_\mathrm{sw}\,V^{2}f\) and, because voltage and frequency are already known, computes its own power estimate from activity counters. It folds thermal sensors into the same loop, because leakage depends on temperature. And it puts cache data arrays behind leakage-suppression transistors, so that only the ~10% of an array in use is awake. He hedged that this might not hold for Esperanto's part, only for anything from a more mature company. The hedge was right. The table below checks six of his statements, paraphrased, against the firmware source, the open RTL and the card.

Terms. DVFS moves the clock and the supply voltage together, here among three operating points: 600, 700 and 800 MHz. Each operating point is one row of the firmware's frequency-to-voltage table, the VMIN-LUT. The compute cores are minions, small in-order RISC-V cores with two hardware threads (harts) each; 32 minions form a shire, and kernels run on 32 shires (1,024 minions). The master minion, firmware on the master shire, dispatches kernels and tells the loop whether the card is busy. The service processor (SP) is the on-die management core that runs the loop. The PMIC is the board's power-management controller, which meters power, and the PVT sensors are the on-die temperature and voltage monitors. TDP is the firmware's power limit, 65 W. The flip-counting model is the Horace experiment's model of power from the bits the multiply-add unit clocks and toggles. More in the hub's glossary.

1. The loop as built

In the et-platform firmware source at 353f20e, the governor's decisions are one function, check_power_throttle_conditions() in ServiceProcessorBL2/services/thermal_pwr_mgmt.c, called once per pass of the device-management task. (The cards' own log lines show that they run an older build of this governor; §8 says what differs.) Reduced to its logic:

once per device-management pass, about every 133 ms
(each I2C sensor read waits 1 ms, then the pass sleeps DM_TASK_DELAY_MS = 10 ms):
    T   = pvt_get_minion_avg_temperature()          // on-die thermal sensor, whole degrees
    P   = pmic_read_average_soc_power()             // MEASURED board power, from the PMIC over I2C
    if  active_power_management and master minion is BUSY:
        if   T > 65 °C       and f > f_min:  step DOWN one VMIN-LUT point
        elif P > 65 W        and f > f_min:  step DOWN one VMIN-LUT point
        elif P < 65 W        and f < f_max:  step UP   one VMIN-LUT point
    on master minion going IDLE:
        set frequency back to the boot point

Four things follow, and all four show up in the measurements below.

  • The input is a measurement, not an estimate. pmic_read_average_soc_power() is a read of the power-management controller's own averaged wattage. Nothing in the loop counts bus bits, instructions or activity factors, in this source or in the older one. Kanter's \(P = C_\mathrm{sw}V^{2}f\) is not evaluated anywhere in this firmware, because the chip does not need to infer the left-hand side: it has a meter.
  • Thermal has priority. The temperature test comes first, and it is a plain if, so when the die reads above 65 °C the power branch is never reached. This card usually idles above 65 °C (at 73 °C on 22 September, even after about 20.6 hours with no work), and there it sits at its lowest operating point however little power it draws.
  • There is no hysteresis. The 353f20e source defines UPPER_POWER_THRESHOLD_GUARDBAND and LOWER_POWER_THRESHOLD_GUARDBAND, a ±5% band around the TDP level, and then never uses them. Both tests compare against the raw thresholds, and the temperature test has no band of any kind: it fires at a whole-degree reading of 66 °C and stops firing at 65 °C.
  • The clock returns to the boot point when the master minion reports idle. The service processor then sets the boot frequency (go_to_idle_state_and_update_pwr_status()). Back-to-back launches rarely trigger it: the zeros runs held 800 MHz across twelve or more consecutive launches of about 0.37 s, 1–2 ms apart.

2. The loop as measured

The governor only raises the clock while the die reads 65 °C or less (our 10 Hz samples caught up-steps at readings of 63 to 66 °C), which in these sessions meant the first runs after a night of idling. Seven such runs exist, from the cool-start session on the morning of 21 September (Horace experiment §6), sampled at 10 Hz. They contain 36 clock transitions. Voltage moved with frequency in every one of them: 600 MHz at 0.517 V, 700 at 0.568, 800 at 0.618. This is real DVFS, not frequency scaling alone.

Run 3, all ones, from a 64 °C die: the clock hunts across the 65 °C threshold, then stays at 600 MHz. Blue: minion clock. Purple: die temperature. Orange: board power.

It hunts, and the missing hysteresis explains it. In this run the board never came near 65 W (it peaked at 56 W); what the loop kept crossing was the 65 °C line. At a reading of 66 °C the clock steps down; once the whole-degree reading is back at 65 °C the thermal test no longer fires, the power test sees 38 W < 65 W, and the clock steps back up. The clock changed seven times in 2.4 s, reaching 800 MHz twice, 1.6 s apart. Then, with the die settled at 66 °C, it stayed at 600 MHz for the last 4.5 s. Over the whole 7.4 s run it spent 5.6 s at the bottom point, 1.0 s at 700 MHz and 0.8 s at 800 MHz.

Every down-step, placed against the two thresholds it could have tripped
Wake-up probe: load latency after the line sat untouched, minus its latency with no idle

No down-step is the power branch acting alone. Of the 18, seven were thermal only (the die above 65 °C while the board drew 45–56 W, well under the limit) and four were both at once (random data draws up to 88 W at 800 MHz and 69 W at 700, and heats fast). The other seven cannot be attributed from our telemetry: the die read 65 °C (once 64) and the board 34–56 W, so neither test fired on the values we sampled. One of them (run 1, zeros, at 5.95 s: 800→600 MHz in a single step, 171 ms after a launch boundary) looks like the boot-point reset. The other six are single-point steps in pairs 0.4–0.5 s apart, which looks like the stepping loop, most likely thermal steps on passes where the service processor read 66 °C between our 10 Hz samples. The power branch never fired on its own, because on this card only random data at 700–800 MHz exceeds 65 W, and it takes the die through 65 °C within half a second.

Launches run back to back every 0.37–0.49 s, so every step is within a quarter of a second of some kernel boundary; the distance to the nearest one explains nothing and is not shown.

The loop is also slow to start: the first clock change came 0.39 to 0.99 s after the kernel launched, three to seven passes of the ~133 ms loop. Once moving, it stepped on successive passes; eight up-steps go straight from 600 to 800 MHz between two 100 ms samples, faster than one table point per pass. The 353f20e source does not explain that; the older governor the cards' logs point to (§8) climbs to the top point in one go.

3. The leakage-suppression transistors

Kanter's description was specific: cache data arrays sit behind leakage-suppression transistors, a lookup checks the tags first and un-suppresses only the part of the array it needs, and the cost is a small wake-up latency. The same trick applies at the granularity of whole cores and execution units.

The design has the ports. The open RTL of the neighbourhood (a group of eight minions) carries the full interface: a per-minion sleep control and its acknowledge (pwr_ctrl_min_nsleepin / nsleepout, one bit per minion), a global pair, and per-minion isolation (pwr_ctrl_min_isolate) with level shifters and a power stub that forces the outputs safe while a domain is down. That is a textbook power-gating structure, sized for the granularity he described.

That RTL is the Erbium configuration (one neighbourhood, no shire cache), so it says nothing about the L2 and L3 arrays; for those, the wake-up probe below is the only evidence.

Nothing drives it. In the open configuration the whole interface is tied off, with a comment saying so:

// Power Ctrl - power control handled outside cpu subsystem and this ifce is not used
.pwr_ctrl_glb_nsleepin ('1),   // '1 = never asleep
.pwr_ctrl_min_nsleepin ('1),
.pwr_ctrl_min_isolate  ('0),

And the service-processor firmware, searched both at 353f20e and in the older governor, contains no power-gating control of any kind: its only PWR_CTRL hits are an eMMC controller's bus voltage, and elsewhere in the tree the name appears only in eMMC and PCIe PHY register headers. Whatever drives those pins on the taped-out part, it is not this firmware, and the chip-level netlist is not in the open release.

And the card shows no sign of it. If an array were suppressed once idle, the first access afterwards would pay the wake-up. The probe: put one line at a chosen level, let the minion spin on its cycle counter for a swept interval from zero to 27 ms (0, 1.7 µs, 17 µs … 27 ms) without touching that line, then time a single load of it: same line, same address, only the idle time varying, 20 repeats.

  • L1 and L3: zero. The paired difference between the longest idle and no idle is 0 cycles, for L1 both with the line placed by an evict and with it simply left in place.
  • L2: −11 cycles, and this comes from the baseline, not the idle. With no idle at all the load reads 61 cycles, probably because the line was still settling after the asynchronous evict that placed it. At every idle from 1.7 µs to 27 ms it is a normal 49.5-cycle L2 hit. No wake-up.
  • DRAM: +10.5 cycles, and that one has a known cause: the line's DRAM row has closed, so the next load pays an activate, 11 cycles (tRCD, the activate-to-read delay). Anatomy of a memory access measured that cost (§6), and found that idle rows close within a few microseconds, faster than refresh (every 3.88 µs) alone would close them; the +40 cycles in that report are a row conflict, a different case. Here 12 of the 20 paired differences are +9 to +14 cycles, six are within 6 cycles of zero and two are outliers. The shift is there in full after the shortest idle, 1.7 µs, and does not grow out to 27 ms, which is not how a wake-up would behave.

So on this card, in this firmware, leakage suppression is a capability that ships switched off. The only gating actually working is clock gating, which the same RTL does use aggressively (per lane, per functional unit, with a seven-cycle tail), and which is why an idle-but-powered core costs so little dynamic power and still leaks.

4. What that costs

Leakage as a share of board power, from the fitted model, against Kanter's typical range

A free out-of-sample check. The leakage curve in this chart, \(P_\text{idle}(T)=12.6\,\mathrm{W}+23.3\,\mathrm{W}\,e^{(T-80)/36}\), was fitted on 21 September (the Horace experiment, §8; tabulated in the energy manual, §1). This report opened by sampling the card after it had sat for about hours with no workload (apart from the 4.9 s wake-up probe of §3, five minutes before), on 22 September, in a warmer room: 31.79 ± 0.04 W at 73.0 °C. The curve predicts 31.78 W. A different day, a different ambient, a temperature between the fit's idle readings, and the error is 0.01 W.

Of those 31.8 idle watts, 11.0 W sits on the minion rail alone: a thousand cores doing nothing for over twenty hours. 2.0 W is the SRAM rail, 3.6 W the mesh, and 15.1 W is on no rail sensor at all (DDR, PCIe, regulator conversion).

$$P_\text{idle}(T)\;=\;\underbrace{12.6\,\mathrm{W}}_{\text{temperature-independent}}\;+\;\underbrace{23.3\,\mathrm{W}\;e^{(T-80\,^\circ\mathrm{C})/36\,^\circ\mathrm{C}}}_{\text{leakage}},\qquad \left.\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right|_{80\,^\circ\mathrm{C}}=0.65\ \mathrm{W/^\circ C}$$

Kanter put leakage at 5–30% of a design's power, with 20% common, and noted that parts which clock lower and use slower transistors leak less. This card is above that range whether busy or idle: 36% of a 64 W random-data matmul, 65% of an idle card at 80 °C. Three things make it so, and only the third is the chip's own: (1) it runs at 0.52 V rather than the 0.4 V Esperanto designed for; (2) a desktop chassis idles it at 73–80 °C rather than in server airflow; (3) it carries 128 MB of shire SRAM (Esperanto quotes over 160 MB on die, at Hot Chips 33) with no array power gating in play.

5. What this means for the equation, and for measuring power

  • The equation is right; it is just not what the loop uses. Its activity term is strongly confirmed on this card. Power over idle is close to linear in active minions: 25.6 mW per minion at 256 and 512 active, 26.2 at 768 and 27.0 at 1,024; a line through zero fits 26.5 mW per minion. And a model that counts four classes of switching event inside the multiply-add unit predicts board power across fourteen operand patterns to 0.5 W rms, each pattern predicted by a fit that left it out. The \(V^{2}f\) term is only weakly testable here, because the governor will not hold the high operating point long on a heavy workload. For constant data it predicts \(\bigl(0.618/0.517\bigr)^{2}\times(800/600)=1.91\) for the switching-power ratio between the two points; the cool-start runs give 2.0 for ones (20.6 against 10.3 W over idle) and 2.05 for zeros (3.9 against 1.9 W), 5% and 8% above it, from seven short, uncontrolled runs. A clean test needs a die below 65 °C and a runner that pins the work rate.
  • Kanter's argument that measured power is too expensive to mandate is weaker for this card, though it does not vanish. His reason MLPerf cannot require measured power is that most submitters will not pay the time and energy to instrument a system. This chip is already instrumented, as a load-bearing part of its own safety loop: a PMIC that meters board power, a service processor that reads it on every pass of its management loop, and a management interface that hands the number to the host. The cost of reporting the card's own power here is one query. But MLPerf's measured power is the whole system (host, power supply, cooling), which this meter does not see, and the meter gives one new board reading per ~133 ms pass, with the rail readings behind the PMIC's own running average (a time constant of about one second).
  • But the loop's hunting is exactly the kind of thing a provisioned-power metric cannot see. A normalisation by provisioned kilowatts scores a card by its nameplate. On this card a governor with no dead band moves the clock, and so the throughput, by up to a third within a single 7-second run, invisibly.
  • The hunting measured here is at the thermal threshold, which has no dead band in either version of the source. The two power-guardband macros that the 353f20e source leaves unused (the older governor uses one of them) would not damp it; damping it needs a temperature dead band.

6. The same firmware on three cards

Everything above is one card. AI Foundry has three machines, and the loop's behaviour turned out to depend on a single byte that differs between them.

aifoundry1 could not be measured. It holds two ET-SoC-1 cards, both enumerated on the PCIe bus, with their device nodes present. Its kernel module is built from different sources than the other two machines (srcversion 1383B256EB24A0A53F04CC7 against 47D26A305A0428B29FB7FC4) while carrying the same libDM.so, and the library's compatibility check refuses to open the card: Error unable to evaluate compatibility!. Its management service is not running. Reinstalling a kernel driver on a shared lab machine is not ours to do, so those two cards are reported here as unavailable and the lab admin has what they need to fix it.

aifoundry2 and aifoundry3 look identical and are not. Same firmware release, same PMIC firmware, same static device configuration over the driver's ioctl: 65 W TDP, 600 MHz boot clock, 32 compute shires, 32 MB of L3. But asked the same question over the management interface, the two service processors give different answers.

The governor compares measured SoC power against that TDP level. With the level at zero, the comparison in check_power_throttle_conditions() can only come out one way:

$$\underbrace{P_\text{measured} > P_\text{TDP}}_{\text{step down}}\;\equiv\;P_\text{measured} > 0\;\equiv\;\text{always true},\qquad \underbrace{P_\text{measured} < P_\text{TDP}}_{\text{step up}}\;\equiv\;P_\text{measured} < 0\;\equiv\;\text{never true}$$

So on aifoundry3 the loop is not disabled. Each kernel start logs a power throttle-down request and each return to idle an idle event, and the step-up request, the only way above 600 MHz, can never fire: the clock read 600 MHz in all 7,745 samples of the session. The service processor's own log, read back out of the card, shows the pattern (six consecutive lines, verbatim):

Power idle state event, current pwr 26220  tdp level 0
Power throttle down event, current pwr 35380  tdp level: 0
Power idle state event, current pwr 26100  tdp level 0
Power throttle down event, current pwr 40200  tdp level: 0
Power idle state event, current pwr 26060  tdp level 0
Power throttle down event, current pwr 48470  tdp level: 0

A second, independent reading agrees. get_power_state() classifies the card as MAX_POWER whenever power exceeds the TDP level and MANAGED_POWER when it is merely above the 30 W safe threshold. aifoundry3 reports max_power while sitting at 23 W and 600 MHz, which is only consistent with a TDP level below 23 W; aifoundry2, drawing more, reports managed_power. Two different code paths, one cause.

The host cannot see this. The driver's ETSOC1_IOCTL_GET_DEVICE_CONFIGURATION reports a 65 W TDP on all three machines, including aifoundry3. Anything that checks a card's configuration from the host side gets the nameplate number, not the one the governor is using. The card works. It is simply held at 600 MHz for as long as its flashed TDP stays at zero, which on a workload that would otherwise run at 800 MHz gives up a quarter of the throughput (800 MHz is a third faster than 600), and nothing reports an error.

The contrast is not that aifoundry2 is always faster. aifoundry2 usually idles above 65 °C, and the thermal branch comes first, so it too sits at 600 MHz most of the time: on 22 September it idled at 72–73 °C and read 600 MHz throughout a thirteen-minute watch. The difference is the reason and the remedy. aifoundry2 reaches 700 and 800 MHz when it starts cool, which is what section 2's 36 transitions are. aifoundry3 cannot, at any temperature, because the branch that would raise it is unreachable.

This also settles what section 2 could not: whether the power branch works at all. It never fired on its own in any aifoundry2 run. On aifoundry3 it is the only branch that fires, at every kernel start, on a die well below the thermal threshold, and it does what the comparison says it will.

7. What the second card says about the model

A card pinned at its lowest operating point is still a card, and running the same strict protocol on it is the strongest out-of-sample test of the flip-counting model: different silicon, different heatsink, a launch temperature 25 °C lower, and a model whose coefficients were fitted entirely on aifoundry2. The same comparison, with the transfer test in full, is §10 of the Horace experiment.

Switching power over idle, eight operand patterns, two cards

Operand patterns: zeros; sparse50, half the entries zero and half random normal; ones; pi, π in every entry; signs, ±1 at random (only the sign bit varies); mant, 1 plus a uniform random fraction (only the mantissa varies); uniform, random uniform on [0, 1); randn, random normal.

The operating point does not explain the gap. aifoundry3 runs its minions at 523 mV against aifoundry2's 518 mV, at the same 600 MHz. By \(P=C_\mathrm{sw}V^{2}f\) that predicts about 2% more switching power on aifoundry3, not 8% less. Whatever the 8% is (silicon, package, the board's regulator efficiency at that load), it is a property of the card, and one number captures it.

Leakage transfers too, and further than it should. The idle law, fitted to aifoundry2's idle readings from 64 to 88 °C (it also passes through a 62 °C overnight reading) and extrapolated 7 to 14 °C below that onto the other card, which idled at 50 to 57 °C, predicts its idle power to within a watt:

The consequence for anyone with a shelf of these cards: the shape of the data-dependence is a property of the design and moves between cards unchanged, while the absolute scale and the idle offset are per-card constants, here 8% and 0.7 W. One random-data matmul on a new card calibrates the scale, and idle readings give the offset.

8. Method, and what is not established

  • Firmware claims are read from the et-platform source at 353f20e: services/thermal_pwr_mgmt.c, rtos_task/dm_task.c, include/thermal_pwr_mgmt.h, include/bl2_pmic_controller.h, config/mgmt_build_config.h.
  • The cards run an older governor than that source. The service processor on aifoundry3 prints log lines that exist only in et-platform firmware before commit 60b40c10f (24 September 2024), which rewrote this governor. In that older governor the power guardband is used, throttle events are logged once per state change, and a step-up climbs to the top point in one go. Both cards report release 1.3.1, so §1 describes the current source, not necessarily the governor on these cards. The measurements in §2–§7 stand either way; the explanations drawn from the source in §1 and §5 may not.
  • RTL claims are read from core-et's Erbium branch at b38a1a3: rtl/shire/neigh/neigh_top.v, neigh_top_pwrstub.v, rtl/cpu_subsystem/cpu_subsystem_top.v. That is the same Minion core lineage in a later MCU-class configuration, not the taped-out ET-SoC-1, which is why "nothing drives the sleep pins" is a statement about the open release and this firmware, corroborated by the card, not a statement about the silicon's chip-level netlist.
  • The 36 transitions come from the cool-start runs of 21 September, in docs/reports/data/2026-09-21-horace-aifoundry2/cold1 and cold2. The classification into thermal, thermal+power, power and unattributed is mechanical, in tools/ettelem/analyze_dvfs.py; nothing is fitted. Our 10 Hz sampling and the sensor's whole-degree steps mean that a step recorded at 66 °C may have been decided at 65 °C, and one recorded at 65 °C may have been decided on a 66 °C reading between our samples. That is the likeliest reading of the seven unattributed steps, and it is why the exact crossing instants are not known.
  • The wake-up probe is gen_ops.py wakeup in workloads/memprobe, one hart, 20 repeats, raw results in docs/reports/data/2026-09-22-dvfs-aifoundry2/wakeup/. It can only detect a wake-up longer than about ten cycles, and only for arrays idled up to 27 ms, which is the widest the delay op encodes. A gating policy that waits longer than that would not show up.
  • The long-idle sample is docs/reports/data/2026-09-22-dvfs-aifoundry2/idle_20h.jsonl.gz: 300 samples over 60 s. The idle time is computed from timestamps, from the end of the last launch of the previous day's long runs to the first sample.
  • The three-machine comparison uses two read-only tools written for it. tools/etcfg issues ETSOC1_IOCTL_GET_DEVICE_CONFIGURATION to the driver; ettelem config asks the service processor for DM_CMD_GET_MODULE_STATIC_TDP_LEVEL, ..._TEMPERATURE_THRESHOLDS and ..._POWER_STATE. Neither writes anything. The firmware log excerpts are the card's own trace buffer, read with ettelem sptrace and stored verbatim in docs/reports/data/2026-09-22-cards/.
  • aifoundry3's session is the same strict protocol as aifoundry2's, targeted at 55 °C instead of 80 °C because that card idles cooler: 20 recorded runs over eight operand patterns, launched at 55.77 ± 0.18 °C. Raw data in docs/reports/data/2026-09-22-horace-aifoundry3/. The model coefficients applied to it are the aifoundry2 fit, unchanged; the only number derived from aifoundry3 is the single scale factor, and the leave-one-out figure shows what it costs to calibrate that on one operand pattern.
  • Not established: whether the taped-out ET-SoC-1 drives its sleep transistors under some other firmware or in some other power state; exactly which governor build the cards run, beyond the log-format evidence above; what the boot frequency is set to in flash; why aifoundry3's flashed TDP is zero, and whether it was ever different; and whether the 8% switching-power gap between the two cards is silicon, package or board regulator, which would need a third working card to separate.
