Why is the ET-SoC-1 low power?

21 September 2026 · aifoundry2's card · A100 figures from NVIDIA's datasheet and Horace He's matmul measurements; Esperanto's design argument from Hot Chips 33 and IEEE Micro · part of the ET-SoC-1 measurement reports

An A100 draws 330 to 400 W under a matrix multiply. This card draws 38 to 64 W, and Esperanto advertised the chip at under 20 W. Both chips are TSMC 7 nm. Esperanto's own explanation is one line, \(P = C_\mathrm{dynamic}\,V^{2}f + P_\mathrm{leakage}\), with every term pushed down. This report measures each term on a real card and takes them away one at a time.

The short answer: the ET-SoC-1 is low power mostly because it runs at 0.52 V and 600 MHz where an A100 runs near 0.85 V and 1,160–1,410 MHz (a factor of 5 to 6 in switching power for the same capacitance), because whatever is not computing is clock-gated (an integer loop on all 1,024 cores, which Esperanto calls minions, costs 1.5 W), and because it does 14 to 28 times fewer FLOPs per second (fp16 or fp32 here, against the A100's bf16). It is not more efficient per FLOP at dense matmul: the A100 spends 1.3 pJ per bf16 FLOP at the board, this card 3.3 pJ in fp16 and 7.0 pJ in fp32. And the term Esperanto's slide leaves without a number, leakage, is the largest single item on this card: 23 W at 80 °C. Clock gating stops idle circuits switching, but no power gating was found on this card, so they still leak.

Terms. The ET-SoC-1's compute cores are minions: small in-order RISC-V cores, 32 to a shire, each shire with 4 MB of SRAM that these cards split into a 512 KB L2 cache, a 1 MB slice of the chip-wide L3 and a 2.5 MB scratchpad. A minion's tensor unit multiplies tiles with TensorFMA, a matrix multiply-accumulate instruction (one fp32 op is 4,096 multiply-adds), fed by TensorLoad, a bulk load of operands into the minion's L1 scratchpad. The service processor's firmware runs a governor that sets the clock and voltage (600, 700 or 800 MHz), and the board's power-management controller (PMIC) meters the 12 V input and three rails: minion, SRAM and NoC, the on-chip mesh network. The Horace experiment is this project's repeat, on this card, of Horace He's test of matrix multiplies on predictable against random data; its power and thermal models are used here. 1. The comparison

Same process, 44% of the transistors, 69% of the die, and a fifth of the power. But also a twenty-eighth of the matmul throughput in fp32 (a fourteenth in fp16), so per FLOP the A100's bf16 tensor cores win by 5.4× against fp32 and 2.6× against fp16.

Against the A100's fp32 CUDA-core datasheet figure instead (19.5 TFLOPS at 400 W, 20.5 pJ per FLOP, no tensor cores), this card's 7.0 pJ is about 2.9× better; the matmul efficiency report, whose faster kernel ran ±1 and ±2 operands at 57 W, puts it at 3.4×. The verdict depends on which precision the GPU is allowed. Per transistor and per square millimetre the ET-SoC-1 really is cooler, by 2.3 and 3.6 times. The rest of this report asks where that comes from.

2. Esperanto's equation, measured

At Hot Chips 33 Esperanto set the target as six chips on a 120 W card: under 20 W per chip, half of it for the thousand minions, "so only 10 mW per core". Their slide compares a generic x86 server core (7 W, 3 GHz, 0.85 V, 2.2 nF of switched capacitance) with the minion they needed (0.01 W, 1 GHz, 0.425 V, 0.04 nF): a reduction of 3× in frequency ("easy"), 4× in V² ("hard": circuits and SRAM) and 58× in capacitance ("very hard": architecture). The card lets each term be read off. Dividing a workload's power over idle by 1,024 minions, V² and f gives its effective switched capacitance per minion:

Esperanto's target row: by P/(V²f), 10 mW at 0.425 V and 1 GHz is 0.055 nF, so the slide's 0.040 nF would leave about 3 mW for the leakage its equation includes. The measured rows are power over idle, which leaves leakage out.

  • Capacitance: the architecture term holds. An integer loop switches 0.009 nF per minion, a fully gated tensor op 0.012 nF, int8 multiply-adds on random data 0.061 nF. The slide's 0.04 nF sits in the middle of the chip's intended workloads. Only floating-point multiply-adds on random data, which the chip was not designed around, reach 0.15 to 0.17 nF.
  • 10 mW per core: the switching part fits for int8, even at this card's higher voltage: 4 mW over idle on constant data and 10 mW on random data. But Esperanto's 10 mW was the whole budget, leakage included, and this card's minion rail alone reads 22 W under int8 random data at the end of a 7 s run (about 82 °C), 21.7 mW per minion. fp32 on random data switches 27 mW per core.
  • Voltage and clock: this card is not at the advertised point. Its lowest operating point is 0.52 V at 600 MHz. While the die reads 65 °C or less (whole degrees) and board power is under 65 W, the firmware steps it up through 0.57 V at 700 MHz to 0.62 V at 800 MHz (see the DVFS loop, and the leakage). Esperanto's "about 0.4 V, 20 W" is a different operating point from anything this card's firmware uses.

Power over idle against the number of active minions
Energy per multiply-add by precision and data
  • The general-purpose side is nearly free. 1,024 minions spinning in an integer loop add 1.46 W to the idle card: 1.4 mW per core, 8 pJ per instruction. That loop, four adds and a branch, issues about 0.3 instructions per cycle on hart 0; the energy manual's tighter addi loop draws 2.1 mW per minion on one hart and 3.4 mW on both, and a nop or a fence costs 4.5–5.3 pJ per issue slot. Esperanto's claim that RISC-V compatibility costs little is borne out. During a tensor instruction the integer pipeline sleeps; a fully gated TensorFMA (zeros) costs 1.9 W for all cores.
  • Power is close to linear in active cores: 25.6 mW per minion at 256 and 512 active, 26.2 at 768 and 27.0 at 1,024; a line through zero fits 26.5 mW per minion. Idle cores cost nothing measurable; there is no cliff and no floor beyond the card's idle.
  • Gating follows the data. At the same FLOPs the fp32 matmul adds 1.9 W on zeros, 10.6 W on ones and 27.6 W on random values. A multiply-add whose operand is zero gets no valid bit and clocks no register; a constant clocks registers but toggles no data. The Horace experiment predicts these from the RTL to 0.5 W rms (section 3), and 14 structured matrices priced before they ran to 0.9 W rms (section 9).
  • Precision is the biggest lever inside the chip. On random data an int8 multiply-add costs 0.32 pJ over idle, fp16 2.7 pJ and fp32 6.0 pJ: a factor of 19 between int8, the type the chip was built for, and fp32. The int8 unit also runs 6.9 times more multiply-adds per second (51 per cycle per minion against 7.5).

Voltage and clock

Esperanto's modelled chip power against core voltage (Hot Chips 33), and this card's measured board power
Leakage: idle board power against die temperature, with the fitted exponential
  • The card has three operating points (600, 700 and 800 MHz); the two ends differ as CV²f says. From a cool die the firmware's clock governor runs kernels at 800 MHz and 0.62 V; past 65 °C, or 65 W at the board, it steps back down to 600 MHz and 0.52 V. The same kernels at nearly the same temperature (64 to 68 °C) draw, over idle: zeros 3.9 against 1.9 W, ones 20.6 against 10.3 W, random fp32 about 53 against 27 W. The 800 MHz figures are the highest readings before the governor stepped down; random data held 800 MHz for at most 0.3 s. That is about 2.0× the switching power for 1.33× the clock; V²f predicts 1.90×. Energy per operation rises 1.5× for 33% more speed.
  • Idle pays for voltage too: 35.0 W at 0.62 V against 28.1 W at 0.52 V, both at 65 °C, a quarter more for a tenth of a volt, from leakage and the always-running clocks.
  • Extrapolated to a GPU's operating point, the same silicon is a GPU-class part. Random fp32 switches 27.6 W here; at 0.85 V and 1,410 MHz the same capacitance would switch 175 W, before leakage, which also grows with voltage. Esperanto's own model is in the same range: about 230 W at 0.85 V, 164 W at 0.75 V. This card's measured board power at 0.52 V, 47 W on ones and 64 W on random fp32, brackets the 44 to 49 W the curve gives there (depending on how its published points are interpolated), although the curve is chip power at its own clock and workload.

Leakage and temperature

  • Idle board power follows \(12.6\,\mathrm{W} + 23.3\,\mathrm{W}\,e^{(T-80\,^\circ\mathrm{C})/36\,^\circ\mathrm{C}}\) from 64 to 88 °C, to 0.2 W rms (the idle law). At 80 °C that is 23 W of leakage in a 36 W idle; at 62 °C, where the card settled after the night of 20–21 September, 14 W in 27 W. After about 20.6 hours without a workload (apart from a 5 s probe five minutes before), on a warmer 22 September, it sat at 73 °C and 31.8 W, which this law predicts to within 0.01 W.
  • This is the term that keeps the measured card from Esperanto's headline. The minion, SRAM and NoC rails alone read 22 W with every multiply-add gated, at 80 °C. A cooler die and the 0.4 V operating point would both cut it: leakage falls exponentially with temperature and steeply with voltage.
  • It also makes the card thermally fragile: each degree adds 0.65 W at 80 °C, and on this card's cooling each sustained watt adds about 1.5 °C, a loop gain near one (0.95) at 80 °C. The Horace experiment's section 8 turns that into a budget: about 3 W of sustained switching before the die runs away to 90 °C in 21 September's room, and 0.3 to 0.7 W under 22 September's warmer conditions.

Memory

  • Streaming tensors from LPDDR4x at 75.5 GB/s, more than half of the datasheet's 137 GB/s (63% of the 119 GB/s that these cards' DDR clock allows), adds 10.7 W: 142 pJ per byte (18 pJ per bit) end to end (DRAM, PHY, controller, mesh and cache fills), on a buffer whose contents were never set. The energy manual's later measurements put a DRAM byte at 122 pJ for 1 KB tensor loads over a buffer likewise never written (its per-level probe, section 4.2), and at 91 (zeros) to 129 (random) pJ for tensor loads of known data: 11–16 pJ per bit.
  • Streaming from the shire's own L2 cache at 2.45 TB/s adds 6.2 W: 2.6 pJ per byte (0.3 pJ per bit), as the energy manual's L2 row (2.5 pJ per byte) confirms.
  • The figures in this section come from two 7 s strict-start runs per configuration at 80 °C; the energy manual, section 4, has the later measurements, with bars from repeated passes on two cards.
  • An A100 moves 1,555 to 2,039 GB/s, eleven to fifteen times more. Esperanto's choice trades bandwidth per chip for power, cost and capacity, and gets bandwidth back by using six chips per card.

4. Putting the terms together

Take the switching power of each chip under its matmul and split it with the equation. The A100 measured by Horace He draws 330 W under load and 88 W idle, so about 242 W switches, at about 0.85 V and 1,160–1,410 MHz: 237–288 nF of effective capacitance. This card's fp32 matmul switches 27.6 W at 0.52 V and 600 MHz: 172 nF.

So of the 8.8× lower switching power, most is the operating point: 6.3× at the A100's maximum clock (2.7× from V², 2.35× from the clock), about 5.2–5.5× at the 1,160–1,230 MHz its capped random-data run implies. The rest, 1.4–1.7×, is switching less capacitance per cycle.

Against the A100 it switches a comparable amount of capacitance every cycle. It does so at a third of the V² and at 43–52% of the clock, which is the 5–6× of the headline, and everything idle is clock-gated to nearly no switching power. What it does not have is efficiency per FLOP on dense floating-point matmul: its fp32 multiply-adds switch nine times more capacitance per FLOP than the A100's bf16 tensor cores do (four times in fp16), and at 9 TFLOPS its idle floor is spread over very little work. On the workload it was designed for, int8 with sparse memory access, the arithmetic is 19 times cheaper than fp32 and the comparison would be much closer; that was not measured against a GPU here.

5. Caveats

  • No A100 was measured here. Its numbers are the datasheet's and Horace He's (one GPU, bf16 8192³ matmul, a 330 W limit). Its core voltage is not published; 0.85 V is an assumption, and the table gives the range. Nor is its clock under the 330 W cap: 1,410 MHz is an upper bound.
  • Everything is board power, which includes LPDDR4x, regulators and PCIe. Late in a run random data draws 29.5 W more than zeros, and about 7 W of that is on no rail sensor; by the later fit in Limits of observability, section 4.2, about 4.4 W of that is regulator delivery loss, and the rest is not attributed. Esperanto's 20 W is a chip figure at an operating point this card's firmware does not offer.
  • The 800 MHz figures come from seven short runs in which the governor changed the clock within seconds. Board power updates about every 133 ms, and one telemetry sample reads its fields over about 22 ms. They agree with V²f to 5%, and deserve a dedicated run from a cool die (tools/ettelem/run_vf_cold.sh is written for it). The die gets below 65 °C only after hours idle in a cool room: after 20.6 hours on a warmer day it was still at 73 °C.
  • The card sits in a desktop chassis: left alone it settled at 62 °C one night and at 73 °C on a warmer day, and every run here launched at 80 °C. In a server airflow it would run cooler and leak less.
  • One card. The second working card, aifoundry3, switches about 8% less power for the same work (the DVFS report, section 7; the Horace experiment, section 10), so the capacitances here are this card's.

6. Method and sources

  • Every configuration ran twice for 7 s under the strict start of the Horace experiment (heat to 84 °C if below, idle to the 81→80 °C step, launch), in shuffled order, with 10 Hz telemetry. Power is the mean over seconds 1 to 3 moved to the launch temperature with the measured leakage slope. Repeats agree to 0.05 W for most configurations.
  • Commands: tools/ettelem/run_ablation.sh build/ablation1 tools/ettelem/ablation.cfg 2 7, then tools/ettelem/analyze_ablation.py. tools/ettelem/finish_horace.sh rebuilds this page and the Horace experiment from the data kept in the repository. Measurements used tools/ettelem, workloads/sparsity and the RTL flip counts of rtl-sim/fma_toggle.
  • Data, all in docs/reports/data/2026-09-21-horace-aifoundry2/: ablation.json and ablation.txt (every configuration's runs), vf.json (the 600 against 800 MHz runs) and model.json (the idle law and the flip model). Notes and quotes: docs/research/why-low-power.md, written on 20–21 September; where its measured numbers differ, this page supersedes them.
  • Firmware: the governor's source was read at et-platform 353f20e; the card's own trace strings match an older build (before et-platform commit 60b40c10f, 24 September 2024). The three operating points show in this card's telemetry.
  • D. Ditzel et al., "Accelerating ML Recommendation with over a Thousand RISC-V/Tensor Processors on Esperanto's ET-SoC-1 Chip", Hot Chips 33 slides, 2021, and IEEE Micro 42(3), 2022.
