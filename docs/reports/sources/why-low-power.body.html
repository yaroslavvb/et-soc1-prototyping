Why is the ET-SoC-1 low power?

aifoundry2's card, 21 September 2026 · et-platform 353f20e firmware · measurements with tools/ettelem, workloads/sparsity and rtl-sim/fma_toggle; A100 figures from NVIDIA's datasheet and Horace He's matmul measurements; Esperanto's design argument from Hot Chips 33 and IEEE Micro · part of the measurement reports

An A100 draws 330 to 400 W under a matrix multiply. This card draws 38 to 64 W, and Esperanto advertised the chip at under 20 W. Both chips are TSMC 7 nm. Esperanto's own explanation is one line, \(P = C_\mathrm{dynamic}\,V^{2}f + P_\mathrm{leakage}\), with every term pushed down. This report measures each term on a real card and takes them away one at a time. The short answer: the ET-SoC-1 is low power mostly because it runs at 0.52 V and 600 MHz where a GPU runs near 0.85 V and 1,400 MHz (a factor of 6 in switching power for the same capacitance), because whatever is not computing is gated off (an integer loop on all 1,024 cores costs 1.5 W), and because it does 28 times fewer FLOPs per second. It is not more efficient per FLOP at dense matmul: the A100 spends 1.3 pJ per FLOP at the board, this card 7.0 pJ. And the term Esperanto's slide leaves without a number, leakage, is the largest single item on this card: 23 W at 80 °C.

1. The comparison

Same process, 44% of the transistors, 69% of the die, and a fifth of the power. But also a twenty-eighth of the matmul throughput, so per FLOP the GPU wins by five. Per transistor and per square millimetre the ET-SoC-1 really is cooler, by 2.3 and 3.6 times. The rest of this report asks where that comes from.

2. Esperanto's equation, measured

At Hot Chips 33 Esperanto set the target as six chips on a 120 W card: under 20 W per chip, half of it for the thousand minions, "so only 10 mW per core". Their slide compares a generic x86 server core (7 W, 3 GHz, 0.85 V, 2.2 nF of switched capacitance) with the minion they needed (0.01 W, 1 GHz, 0.425 V, 0.04 nF): a reduction of 3× in frequency ("easy"), 4× in V² ("hard": circuits and SRAM) and 58× in capacitance ("very hard": architecture). The card lets each term be read off. Dividing a workload's power over idle by 1,024 minions, V² and f gives its effective switched capacitance per minion:

  • Capacitance: the architecture term holds. An integer loop switches 0.009 nF per minion, a fully gated tensor op 0.012 nF, int8 multiply-adds on random data 0.061 nF. The slide's 0.04 nF sits in the middle of the chip's intended workloads. Only floating-point multiply-adds on random data, which the chip was not designed around, reach 0.15 to 0.17 nF.
  • 10 mW per core: about right for int8, even at this card's higher voltage: 4 mW on constant data and 10 mW on random data. fp32 on random data costs 27 mW per core.
  • Voltage and clock: this card is not at the advertised point. Its lowest operating point is 0.52 V at 600 MHz, and below 65 °C the firmware raises it to 0.62 V at 800 MHz. Esperanto's "about 0.4 V, 20 W" is a different operating point from anything this card's firmware uses.

  • The general-purpose side is nearly free. 1,024 minions spinning in an integer loop add 1.46 W to the idle card: 1.4 mW per core, 8 pJ per instruction. Esperanto's claim that RISC-V compatibility costs little is borne out. During a tensor instruction the integer pipeline sleeps; a fully gated TensorFMA (zeros) costs 1.9 W for all cores.
  • Power is linear in active cores: 25.6 mW per minion at 256, 512 and 768 active, 27.0 at 1,024. Idle cores cost nothing measurable; there is no cliff and no floor beyond the card's idle.
  • Gating follows the data. At the same FLOPs the fp32 matmul adds 1.9 W on zeros, 10.6 W on ones and 27.6 W on random values. A multiply-add whose operand is zero gets no valid bit and clocks no register; a constant clocks registers but toggles no data (the Horace experiment's sections 3 and 9 predict these from the RTL to under a watt).
  • Precision is the biggest lever inside the chip. On random data an int8 multiply-add costs 0.32 pJ over idle, fp16 2.7 pJ and fp32 6.0 pJ: a factor of 19 between the type the chip was built for and the one a GPU benchmark uses. The int8 unit also runs 6.9 times more multiply-adds per second (51 per cycle per minion against 7.5).

Voltage and clock

Esperanto's modelled chip power against core voltage (Hot Chips 33), and this card's measured board power
Leakage: idle board power against die temperature, with the fitted exponential
  • The card has two operating points, and they differ as CV²f says. From a cool die the governor runs kernels at 800 MHz and 0.62 V; past 65 °C it drops to 600 MHz and 0.52 V. The same kernels at nearly the same temperature (64 to 68 °C) draw, over idle: zeros 3.9 against 1.9 W, ones 20.6 against 10.2 W, random fp32 about 52 against 26 W. That is 2.0× the switching power for 1.33× the clock; V²f predicts 1.90×. Energy per operation rises 1.5× for 33% more speed.
  • Idle pays for voltage too: 35.0 W at 0.62 V against 28.1 W at 0.52 V, both at 65 °C, a quarter more for a tenth of a volt, from leakage and the always-running clocks.
  • Extrapolated to a GPU's operating point, the same silicon is a GPU-class part. Random fp32 switches 27.6 W here; at 0.85 V and 1,410 MHz the same capacitance would switch 175 W, before leakage, which also grows with voltage. Esperanto's own model says the same: 164 W at 0.75 V, 275 W at the highest voltage. This card's measured points sit on that curve: it interpolates to about 49 W at 0.52 V, and the board measures 47 W on ones and 64 W on random fp32.

Leakage and temperature

  • Idle board power follows \(12.6\,\mathrm{W} + 23.3\,\mathrm{W}\,e^{(T-80\,^\circ\mathrm{C})/36\,^\circ\mathrm{C}}\) from 64 to 88 °C, to 0.2 W. At 80 °C that is 23 W of leakage in a 36 W idle; at 62 °C, where the card settles after a night idle, 14 W in 27 W.
  • This is the term that keeps the measured card from Esperanto's headline. The minion, SRAM and mesh rails alone read 22 W with every multiply-add gated, at 80 °C. A cooler die and the 0.4 V operating point would both cut it: leakage falls exponentially with temperature and steeply with voltage.
  • It also makes the card thermally fragile: each degree adds 0.65 W, and on this card's cooling each sustained watt adds about 1.5 °C, a loop gain near one at 80 °C. The Horace experiment's section 8 turns that into a budget: about 3 W of sustained switching before the die runs away to 90 °C.

Memory

  • Streaming tensors from LPDDR4x at 75 GB/s, more than half of the chip's 137 GB/s, adds 10.7 W: 18 pJ per bit end to end (DRAM, PHY, controller, mesh and cache fills). Streaming from the shire's own L2 SRAM at 2.4 TB/s adds 6.2 W: 0.3 pJ per bit.
  • An A100's HBM2e moves 1,555 GB/s, eleven times more. Esperanto's choice trades bandwidth per chip for power, cost and capacity, and gets bandwidth back by using six chips per card.

4. Putting the terms together

Take the switching power of each chip under its matmul and split it with the equation. The A100 measured by Horace He draws 330 W under load and 88 W idle, so about 242 W switches, at about 0.85 V and 1,410 MHz: 237 nF of effective capacitance. This card's fp32 matmul switches 27.6 W at 0.52 V and 600 MHz: 172 nF.

So the ET-SoC-1's low power is three parts operating point and one part doing less. Against the A100 it switches a comparable amount of capacitance every cycle. It does so at a third of the V² and at 43% of the clock, which is the 6.3× of the headline, and everything idle is gated to nearly nothing. What it does not have is efficiency per FLOP on dense floating-point matmul: its general-purpose vector units switch nine times more capacitance per FLOP than tensor cores do, and at 9 TFLOPS its idle floor is spread over very little work. On the workload it was designed for, int8 with sparse memory access, the arithmetic is 19 times cheaper and the comparison would be much closer; that was not measured against a GPU here.

5. Caveats

  • No A100 was measured here. Its numbers are the datasheet's and Horace He's (one GPU, bf16 8192³ matmul, a 330 W limit). Its core voltage is not published; 0.85 V is an assumption, and the table gives the range.
  • Everything is board power, which includes LPDDR4x, regulators (about 7 W of the 28 W under random data never reaches the die) and PCIe. Esperanto's 20 W is a chip figure at an operating point this card's firmware does not offer.
  • The 800 MHz figures come from seven short runs in which the governor changed the clock within seconds, read with telemetry whose fields are sampled a few milliseconds apart. They agree with V²f to 5%, and deserve a dedicated run from a cool die (tools/ettelem/run_vf_cold.sh is written for it; the die needs hours idle to get below 65 °C).
  • The card sits in a desktop chassis and idles at 62 to 80 °C. In a server airflow it would run cooler and leak less.

6. Method and sources

  • Every configuration ran twice for 7 s under the strict start of the Horace experiment (heat to 84 °C if below, idle to the 81→80 °C step, launch), in shuffled order, with 10 Hz telemetry. Power is the mean over seconds 1 to 3 moved to the launch temperature with the measured leakage slope. Repeats agree to 0.05 W for most configurations.
