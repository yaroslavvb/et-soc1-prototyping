The Horace experiment: the same matmul draws 38 to 63 W depending on the data

20–22 September 2026 · aifoundry2 (sections 1–9) and aifoundry3 (section 10) · fp32 TensorFMA on all 1,024 minions · workloads/sparsity, tools/ettelem, rtl-sim/fma_toggle · part of the ET-SoC-1 measurement reports

Horace He's post "Strangely, Matrix Multiplications on GPUs Run Faster When Given 'Predictable' Data!" (April 2024) found that an A100 multiplies matrices of zeros about 15% faster than random ones (295 against 257 TFLOPS): predictable data flips fewer transistors, draws less power, and a power-limited GPU then clocks higher. On this chip the whole chain can be taken apart, because the RTL (the Verilog source) of its core family's multiply-add unit is open. At the same clock and the same 9.2 TFLOPS, a matmul on aifoundry2's card draws 38 W on zeros, 47 W on ones and 63 W on random values (aifoundry3's card, launched 25 °C cooler, draws 27, 35 and 50 W, in the same order). It heats the die roughly in proportion to the watts above idle (12 and 30 W): 27 and 76 thousandths of a degree per trillion FLOPs on ones and random values (21 and 70 on aifoundry3), with random data heating 8 to 26% more per watt than ones. On zeros (2 W above idle on both cards) the whole-degree reading moves by at most one step during a run, so the rise is not resolved: below about 1 °C (0.1 to 0.3 °C by the recovered curve and the thermal network). It follows the sparse compute report, whose first two runs, on aifoundry3, found that zeros save most of the tensor unit's power but no cycles.

Simulating that RTL (core-et's Erbium branch: the same Minion core lineage in a later configuration, not the taped-out netlist) on the operands the card ran explains the watts: register bits clocked and nets toggled predict the board power of 14 patterns to 0.5 W rms, each predicted by a fit that left it out; six of them had been predicted from the previous day's data before they first ran in the session of 21 September, to 1.3 W rms. A thermal network fitted to the same session then turns watts into the temperature curve to about 0.3 °C rms, which is what the sensor's rounding to whole degrees alone would leave (two stages on aifoundry2; aifoundry3's own network reaches the same floor with more). Horace's speed effect itself needs a die cool enough for the governor to raise the clock; section 6 shows it in a demonstration of seven runs on aifoundry2, whose firmware, unlike aifoundry3's, lets the clock rise.

Run for minutes instead of seconds, only zeros hold a steady temperature at full load (aifoundry2, one four-hour session): random data takes the die from 80 to 90 °C in 19 to 26 s (four runs), ones in about two minutes (three runs). A three-line model (flip rates to watts, leakage that grows with temperature, a thermal network) predicts those times from the flip counts. On the twelve later runs of that session, which it was not fitted to, the time to 90 °C is off by 9% in the median and 23% at worst (the seven runs that reached it); at ten minutes it runs 3 to 5 °C hot on workloads near the card's flip budget. It priced 14 structured matrices, from Hadamard to kaleidoscope, to 0.9 W rms before they ran. On a second card, aifoundry3, the power model is off by a single factor, 0.92, which a calibration on random data recovers in that card's one session; the thermal network does not transfer.

Terms used on this page

The compute cores of the ET-SoC-1 (Esperanto's RISC-V accelerator, now open-sourced by AI Foundry) are minions, each with two hardware threads (harts) and a tensor unit whose matrix multiply-add instruction is TensorFMA (one fp32 op multiplies 16×16×16 tiles: 4,096 multiply-adds); 32 minions form a shire. The service processor is the on-die management core whose firmware reads the sensors and runs the clock governor (600, 700 or 800 MHz). aifoundry2 and aifoundry3 are the lab machines that hold the two cards measured. 1. Same FLOPs, different heat

All 1,024 minions (the chip's compute cores, 32 to a shire; the chip has 1,088 in 34 shires, and the two not used here are the master and spare shires) each run one 16×16×16 fp32 TensorFMA (the tensor unit's matrix multiply-add) over and over, on operands held in the L1 scratchpad (3 KB of each minion's 4 KB data cache). Every pattern takes 546.0 cycles per op at 600 MHz, so every run does the same 9.18 × 10¹² FLOPs per second, 6.7 × 10¹³ in its 7.3 s. Only the values in A and B differ. Six patterns ran five times each; eight more ran twice each for the model in section 3. Sections 1 to 9 are aifoundry2's card; section 10 repeats these 7 s runs on aifoundry3, and where the second card's values differ the text gives both.

  • The runs cluster by kind, tightly. Random normal draws 63.40 W with a run-to-run spread of 0.08 W over five different random matrices; zeros 38.29 ± 0.03 W; ones 46.73 ± 0.07 W. The rises of those three repeat to 0.03 °C (on aifoundry3 to 0.05 to 0.2 °C). They form three groups, 7 to 15 W apart on both cards: zeros alone, 2 W above idle; ones, π and the half-zero random matrix within a watt of each other (46 to 47 W, +1.7 to +1.8 °C); random uniform and random normal within about 2 W (61.3 against 63.4 W, a gap aifoundry2 resolves and aifoundry3 does not).
  • Heating per FLOP follows power over idle, roughly, not total power. Just before each launch the idle card draws 36.3 W and the die is in balance. Averaged over the run, including the leakage its own heating adds, zeros add 2.0 W to that; the reading moves by at most one step, so the rise is only bounded (below about 1 °C; the recovered curve and the thermal network put it at 0.1 to 0.3 °C, 1 to 5 m°C per 10¹² FLOPs). Ones add 11.6 W: 27 m°C. Random normal adds 30.4 W: 76 m°C. (At the launch temperature the three add 2.0, 10.5 and 27.1 W. aifoundry3, idling at 25.1 W, adds 2.0, 10.4 and 27.2 W over its runs: 21 and 70 m°C for ones and random normal.) The proportion is not exact: the ratio of the last two rises, 2.85, exceeds the ratio of the added watts, 2.63 (3.29 against 2.61 on aifoundry3), so random data heat 8% (aifoundry2) and 26% (aifoundry3) more per board watt than ones; section 4 compares the two run by run.
  • In energy, averaged over the 7 s run, a FLOP costs 4.2 pJ of board energy on zeros, 5.2 pJ on ones and 7.3 pJ on random normal (3.0, 3.9 and 5.7 pJ on aifoundry3, whose idle is lower). Over the idle card those are 0.2, 1.3 and 3.3 pJ: the data decides a factor of 15 (14 on aifoundry3). The run average includes the leakage the run's own heating adds (66.7 W mean for random normal); at the launch temperature random normal is 63.4 W ÷ 9.18 TFLOPS = 6.9 pJ per FLOP (aifoundry3: 50.0 W, 5.4 pJ).
  • Which bits are random matters. Random signs alone (±1) and random exponents alone cost about the same, 51.4 and 52.6 W; random mantissas alone 60.4 W, and all three together 63.4 W. One random operand with the other held at 1 costs 54.3 W when A is the random one and 57.9 W when B is. Section 3 shows why.
  • The rails agree on where it goes. Late in the run random normal is 29.6 W above zeros on the board. The minion-core rail's own sensor accounts for 21.5 W of that and the SRAM and mesh rails for 1.0 W between them, about what a die 5 °C hotter adds to their leakage. The remaining 7 W is on no rail sensor, consistent with conversion loss in the regulators, which grows with the current. On aifoundry3 the same difference is 25.9 W: 20.5 W on the minion rail, 0.5 W on the SRAM and mesh rails, 4.9 W on no sensor.

2. Strict temperature control

Two things make temperature a confound on this card. Power at the same work rises with die temperature through leakage: about 0.8 W per °C on the board while a hot pattern runs, measured from the drift of power within the hot 7 s runs (80–86 °C); the idle law's slope is 0.65 W/°C at 80 °C (section 8). And on aifoundry2 the firmware's clock governor has a 65 °C threshold: below it a busy card is clocked up to 800 MHz at 0.62 V, above it the card is held at 600 MHz and 0.52 V (section 6; aifoundry3's firmware never leaves 600 MHz). So every run starts from the same thermal state, well above that threshold:

  1. Heat. If the die is below 84 °C, 2 s bursts of random-data matmul bring it there.
  2. Cool along the same curve. The card idles until the mean of the 34 minion-shire sensors first reads 80 °C. Approached from above, that reading is an edge, not a range: the kernel launches within a second of the 81→80 step.
  3. Run one pattern for 7 s, with 10 Hz telemetry throughout. Each process holds the card for under 10 s.

The control held (the checks are under Method): every sample read 600 MHz and 516–518 mV; at the launches the die was at °C in the sensor's units with W idle, and random normal repeated across the hour's five blocks to W and °C (standard deviations).

The limit that remains is the sensor: it reports whole degrees. A run's rise read off the sensor is good to about half a degree, so the rises in this report are de-quantised: for each run, one number (the heating power) is fitted so that the thermal network of section 4, started from the launch state and rounded like the sensor, reproduces the run's readings. The times at which the reading steps from one degree to the next carry the information. Power needs none of this: it reads in 10 mW steps.

3. From flips to watts

The power differences come mostly from the fused multiply-add units, eight per minion, one per vector lane: the minion rail carries 73% of the difference between random data and zeros on aifoundry2 and 79% on aifoundry3. The open RTL of this core family (core-et, Erbium branch at b38a1a3, a later configuration of the same Minion core lineage) includes that unit (txfma_top, seven pipeline stages) and the control around it. rtl-sim/fma_toggle runs eight copies under Verilator on the exact operand tiles the card ran (the host dumps them), in the order the tensor state machine issues them: for each row of B, for each row of A, two micro-ops of eight c = a·b + c; 512 micro-ops per op. Every result is checked: all 8,192 multiply-adds of two random-data ops match software bit for bit. Three things in the RTL decide the activity:

  • Zero gating. On an accumulate pass, vpu_ctrl.v withholds the valid bit from a lane whose A or B operand word is zero (ex_fma_gate_mask; the manual's pseudo-code says the same, if (a != 0 && b != 0)). The unit's pipeline registers are enabled by their stage's valid bit, so a gated lane clocks no register and flips no net. That is the zeros case: in the simulation nothing in the unit moves, and on both cards a zeros matmul costs only 2 W over idle.
  • Clocked registers. A valid multiply-add clocks about 600 register bits per lane on its way down the pipe, whether or not the data changed. That is what a constant costs: ones and π clock the registers and toggle almost nothing. (The checkerboard keeps a quarter of its lanes: a product needs both operands nonzero.)
  • Data toggles. Random operands flip about 87 million counted nets per op, 85% of them in the multiplier tree (Booth encoders, carry-save adders, 4:2 compressors). Sign-only or exponent-only randomness leaves that tree still and moves only the exponent path, the aligner and the adder.

The table gives the counts per op for one minion, from the card's own tiles. The model is their weighted sum, with one non-negative energy per kind of event, fitted to the 14 measured powers:

board W = constant + e₁ · register bits clocked + e₂ · toggles in the multiplier tree + e₃ · toggles in the rest of the unit + e₄ · operand-word toggles outside the unit

  • It fits to 0.3 W and predicts to 0.5 W. Fitted to all 14 patterns the rms error is 0.32 W over a 25 W range. Refitted 14 times with one pattern left out, the left-out pattern is predicted to 0.50 W rms (the ticks above). The worst are zeros and random exponents (+0.9 W) and ternary (−0.8 W).
  • One energy per toggle does not work. With a single count of all net toggles the error is 2.1 W (2.5 W left out; on aifoundry3, refitted to its own eight patterns and each left out in turn, 2.4 W against 1.3 W for the split form): the multiplier tree holds 85% of the counted toggles of a random multiply and little of its energy. Counted separately, a toggle in the tree costs 0.03 fJ and one in the rest of the unit 0.8 fJ. The count is of named RTL nets. The tree is thousands of one-bit compressor cells whose short local nets are each counted at two or three levels of the hierarchy; the rest of the unit is wide buses, shifters and pipeline registers driving long wires. At 0.52 V, 0.8 fJ per toggle is about 6 fF of switched capacitance.
  • Clocking is the cost of a constant. 2.47 million register bits clocked per op at 2.96 fJ each is 8.2 W; ones measure 8.4 W over zeros (7.8 W on aifoundry3). The figure includes the clock network behind the registers and the write-back of each result, which scale with it.
  • It explains the odd orderings. A random with B = 1 flips 38 million tree nets per op, and A = 1 with B random only 13 million, yet the second draws 3.6 W more: it moves 10.1 million nets in the rest of the unit against 5.9 million, and twice as many operand-word bits. Random signs or exponents leave the tree still (under 0.4 million toggles) and cost 5 to 6 W over ones, all of it in the rest of the unit. A half-zero random matrix gates three quarters of its multiply-adds and still costs within about a watt of ones (0.6 W less on aifoundry2, 0.3 W less on aifoundry3): by the model, because the quarter that runs is fully random.

4. From watts to degrees

The die's temperature follows power through a thermal network. A Foster chain (RC stages in series) with time constants fixed on a grid from 1.5 s to 1,000 s was fitted to the whole hour: board power in, sensor reading out, resistances held non-negative. On aifoundry2 two stages carry everything this session can see.

Die temperature over the whole session: sensor reading (blue) and the thermal network driven by measured board power (orange)

A watt added to the board raises the sensor by 0.10 °C within a couple of seconds (the 1.5 s stage, presumably die and spreader) and by another 0.12 °C over the next half minute (the 10 s stage, presumably package and heatsink base). That is 0.06 °C per W after 1 s, 0.12 after 3 s and 0.16 after 7 s; aifoundry3's own network, fitted the same way to its 13-minute session, needs more stages and gives 0.07 after 1 s and 0.18 after 7 s. The slower stages get nothing only because this protocol holds the hour's average power constant; the model of section 8, fitted to the long runs of section 7 (aifoundry2, one session), which do not, puts °C per W in stages of 60 s and longer ( °C per W in all). The in-sample residual over the hour is 0.28 °C rms, which is what rounding to whole degrees alone would leave (0.29 °C; aifoundry3's own network reaches 0.29 °C on its session too); driven by the measured power over five hours, section 8's model follows the sensor to 0.63 °C.

With both steps in hand, the chain runs from the RTL to the thermometer with no measurement of the run in between. For each run the network starts from the state the hour's measured power had left it in at launch. From there it is driven by the activity model's power for that pattern (from the fit that left that pattern out), plus 0.81 W for every degree the simulated die has gained: leakage, measured from the drift of power within the hot runs.

The ring uses no electrical measurement at all: it is the heating power recovered from the whole-degree steps alone (section 2). For the patterns that heat the die by more than a degree, this thermal estimate agrees with the electrical one to W rms, with a slope of between them (on aifoundry3, with its own network, 2.6 W rms and a slope of 0.93, the thermal estimate reading about a tenth low): the temperature rise is roughly proportional to the power, and the power follows the counted flips. Roughly, because random data heat 8% (aifoundry2) and 26% (aifoundry3) more per board watt than ones (section 1). For the three coolest patterns (2 to 5 W over idle) the reading moves by at most one whole degree during a run (one 75%-zero run read 82 °C in its last half second). That only bounds their rise to below about one degree; the network predicts 0.3 to 0.8 °C.

5. Where the model is off, and why

  1. Most of the power is not flips of this computation. Of random normal's 63.4 W, 38.6 W is the model's constant: what the card draws at 80 °C with every multiply-add gated. Idle is 36.3 W of it: leakage (the same idle card read 26.7 W at 62 °C after the night of 20–21 September, a single reading), always-on clocks, DDR, PCIe and regulator losses. The other 2 W is what a fully gated tensor op costs, on both cards; by the RTL, it is the 1,024 tensor state machines stepping through gated micro-ops. Temperature rises with power over the idle level, so heating per FLOP runs from less than the sensor can resolve on zeros to 76 m°C on random normal (70 on aifoundry3), while energy per FLOP (run averages) only runs from 4.2 to 7.3 pJ (3.0 to 5.7 pJ on aifoundry3).
  2. Register clocking is switching that the data does not control, except by being zero. A constant costs 8.4 W over zeros with almost no net toggles. "Proportional to flips" holds only once clock edges delivered to registers count as flips.
  3. A counted toggle is not a fixed charge. The fitted energies differ 25-fold between the multiplier tree and the rest of the unit. RTL net counts stand in for capacitance only within blocks of similar structure; a netlist with extracted capacitances would replace three fitted numbers with none. The zero-delay simulation also has no glitches. Real carry-save trees glitch, more so on random data, and the fitted energies absorb the average.
  4. Leakage feeds back. Random normal reads 65.8 W in seconds 1 to 3 and 68.0 W in the last two seconds of the same run: + W as its own heating lifts the reading another °C between those windows ( °C over the whole run). The model's watts are at the launch temperature, and the chain adds 0.81 W per simulated degree. Without that term it would under-predict the hot patterns' rise by about a tenth.
  5. The sensor. One whole-degree number stands for the mean of 34 sensors. The thermal fit's residual is the rounding. The launch temperature is known to a few tenths of a degree in absolute terms, the same for every run, which matters only for the three coolest patterns. The hot spots are the multiply-add units, not the sensors, so the junctions that leak are presumably hotter than the reading (no hot spot was measured).
  6. Board power is not die power. About 7 of random normal's 30 W over zeros are on no rail sensor (4.9 of 26 W on aifoundry3). By the later attribution (Limits of observability, §4.2) about 4.4 W of that is consistent with regulator delivery loss, which would never reach the die, and the rest is not attributed. The network was fitted to board power, so it absorbs whatever does not heat the die as fewer °C per W. It would not transfer to a workload that loads a different rail.
  7. What is left, on aifoundry2. Power: 0.5 W rms out of sample, 0.9 W at worst. Rise: 0.33 °C rms from the RTL alone against 0.30 °C from the measured power, both fit residuals of this session (aifoundry3, with its own refit and network: 0.35 against 0.32 °C). So almost all of the temperature error belongs to the thermal step and the sensor, not to the flips.

6. The speed effect on a cool die: a demonstration

This section is one card, aifoundry2, and seven runs on the morning of 21 September: two of zeros, two of ones and three of random normal, in two short sessions; the second card cannot repeat it (below). On aifoundry2 the firmware's governor steps the clock down while the whole-degree die reading is above 65 °C or board power is above 65 W (its TDP setting, the board power limit), so it holds 600 MHz at 0.52 V on a hot die and lifts a cooler busy card to 700–800 MHz (The DVFS loop and its leakage, §1–§2). Every run on 20 September started above 72 °C, so the thermal test held 600 MHz throughout, even at 70 W. After the night of 20–21 September idle the die read 62 °C (one reading), and the same kernels behaved differently:

Minion clock during 7 s runs started from a cool die (62–64 °C): aifoundry2, seven runs on 21 September
  • Zeros keep the fast clock for nearly the whole run (both runs): 800 MHz from about a second after launch for 5 to 6 s, at 39 W, with the die at 64 to 66 °C after 7 s. The governor only starts stepping down in the last second. 11.4 and 11.8 TFLOPS.
  • Random normal loses it within a second. At 800 MHz and 0.62 V it draws up to 88 W for an instant, the die passes 65 °C in half a second, and the governor steps down to 700 and then 600 MHz. 9.3 TFLOPS in all three runs, at 55 W: the die is 15 °C cooler than in section 1, which is worth 10 W of leakage.
  • Ones hover at the threshold (two runs): up to 800 MHz, down at 66 °C, up again as the die falls back to 65 °C. 9.7 TFLOPS.

So in these seven runs predictable data did run faster: 25% for zeros against random values, where Horace measured 15% on an A100. With two zeros runs against three random ones that is a demonstration, not an established effect, though the clock record shows the mechanism in every run. The reason is the one he gave: fewer flips, less power, and a governor that then allows a higher clock. Here the limit that bites first is thermal rather than electrical, and it only applies to a die that starts below 65 °C. A card that has been busy idles above that (72 °C on 20 September; the runs of section 1 were held at 80 °C), runs everything at 600 MHz, and shows the effect as heat instead of speed. Nor are the seven runs a controlled experiment: their start temperatures differ by up to 2 °C, and a die that has just run does not return to 62 °C for hours. The second card of section 10 cannot show the effect at all: its firmware reports a TDP of 0 W, so the governor only ever steps down and the clock never leaves 600 MHz (the DVFS report).

7. Minutes instead of seconds

Seven seconds show the first two stages of the heating. To see the rest, the same patterns ran on aifoundry2 as single processes for up to ten minutes each, in one session on 21 September, from the same start: heat to 84 °C if below, idle until the sensor first reads 80 °C, launch. Most configurations ran once; the counts are given below. A run ends early when the die's mean reading reaches 90 °C, which keeps it inside what this card had already seen (on 20 September the mean reading had reached 90 °C and the hottest sensor 93 °C, at up to 70 W). 29 runs took four hours, because after a hot run the die needs up to seven minutes to get back to 80 °C. Some runs used fewer minions per shire, which scales the flip rate without changing the flips themselves (and the switching power only roughly in proportion: per active minion it is about 5% higher on 1,024 minions than on 256).

The first run (zeros, at the start of the session, with no telemetry before it to set the model's state) and the last two (after the cooling changed) are not in the fit.   • At full load only zeros hold a steady temperature. Random normal reaches 90 °C in 19 to 26 s (four runs); in one run each, random uniform in 22 s, random mantissas in 24 s, random signs in 53 s, π in 104 s and the half-zero random matrix in 107 s. Ones take 107 to 167 s (three runs). Zeros (three runs) cool the die to 76 to 78 °C over ten minutes, by the model because the heatsink is still shedding the heat of the run before. The checkerboard and the 75%-zero random matrix (one run each) last the ten minutes but are at 87 °C and rising.
  • Fewer minions buy time, far more than in proportion (one run at each count, two at 256). Removing a quarter of the flips (768 minions) stretches random normal from 19–26 s to 35 s; removing half (512 minions) to 84 s; three quarters (256 minions) to 276 and 315 s, more than ten times as long; on 128 minions it sits at 81 to 82 °C for the whole ten minutes. Near the flip budget of section 8, each watt removed buys more time than the last.
  • Equal flip power, similar curves. By the flip counts of section 3, ones on all 1,024 minions switch 10.7 W and random normal on 384 minions 10.2 W. The first is all register clocking, the second mostly data toggles, and the times are consistent with equal heating: 107 to 167 s for ones (three runs) against 119 s for random normal on 384 minions (one run), with curves that overlap in the chart above.
  • The same pattern does not always take the same time. Ones needed 107 s after a hot predecessor (one run) and 162 to 167 s after a ten-minute zeros run (two runs). Every run starts at the same die reading, but the heatsink behind it does not start in the same state, and over minutes that matters. The model below carries that state.
  • During the 28th run, a single event, the card's cooling changed abruptly: under an unchanged workload (42.7 W at 84 °C, falling to 35.6 W as it cooled) the die fell from 84 to 72 °C in four minutes; presumably something changed the airflow in the lab. That run and the one after it are in the chart but not in the fits.

8. A model from flips to temperature

With the long runs the chain of sections 3 and 4 closes into one model that needs only the flip schedule. It is aifoundry2's model, fitted to that card's sessions of 21 September, and it has three lines:

$$\textbf{flips}\qquad F_j(t) \;=\; N_j(\text{pattern})\;\times\;1.10\times10^{6}\ \text{ops/s}\;\times\;\text{active minions}$$ $$\textbf{power}\qquad P(t) \;=\; \underbrace{12.6\,\mathrm{W}}_{\text{fixed}} \;+\; \underbrace{23.3\,\mathrm{W}\,e^{(T-80\,^\circ\mathrm{C})/36\,^\circ\mathrm{C}}}_{\text{leakage}} \;+\; \underbrace{1.85\,\mathrm{W}\cdot\tfrac{\text{active}}{1024}}_{\text{state machines}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_j e_j\,F_j(t)}_{\text{switching}}$$ $$\textbf{heat}\qquad T(t) \;=\; 22.8\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \;+\; \sum_k x_k(t), \qquad \tau_k\,\frac{\mathrm{d}x_k}{\mathrm{d}t} \;=\; R_k\,P(t) \;-\; x_k$$

The power line has a fixed part, leakage that grows exponentially with die temperature, the tensor state machines of the active minions, and an energy for each kind of flip. It was fitted to every 10 Hz sample of five hours of sessions: the leakage to the idle samples (rms error 0.20 W from 64 to 88 °C), then the flip energies to the busy samples (0.49 W). The idle samples fix the leakage's slope, 0.65 W per °C at 80 °C, but not how the idle power splits between the fixed part and the exponential: a temperature scale anywhere from 30 to 45 °C in the exponent (the fit above uses 36 °C) fits them as well, with 20 to 29 W of leakage at 80 °C and 16 to 7 W fixed. The flip energies are close to section 3's, which used only seconds 1 to 3 of 46 short runs: 3.18 against 2.96 fJ per register bit and 0.80 against 0.78 fJ per other toggle; the operand-word energy is the loosest (15.5 against 12.5 fJ). These are the values the predictor and the energy manual use. The heat line is the thermal network of section 4 with its slow stages now visible; one more number pins the slowest of them, the 62 °C at 26.7 W where this card had settled after the night of 20–21 September (a single reading). Driven by the measured power it follows the sensor to 0.63 °C rms over the five hours.

Leakage is what makes temperature so sensitive to flips here, whatever its share of the 36 W idle (20 to 29 W at 80 °C, above; two thirds in the best fit). Each degree adds W at 80 °C, and each watt adds °C through the network after ten minutes, °C once settled. (0.65 W/°C is the slope of the idle law. Sections 2 and 4 use 0.81 W per degree, the drift of busy power inside the hot 7 s runs: a different measurement, a quarter higher.) Closed on itself, that loop has a gain of at 80 °C: a watt of switching held for ten minutes raises the die by °C, not , and the gain passes one at 82 °C. How well it fits. For each long run the network is started in the state the measured history had left it in at launch, and then driven by the power line above, with the run's flip rates and its own simulated temperature; nothing measured during the run goes in. The dashed lines in the chart of section 7 are these curves. For the 19 runs that reached 90 °C the time is off by 12% in the median (23 s for 26 s measured, 122 for 119, 326 for 315); the worst is the half-zero random matrix, 157 s for 107 s. These are fit numbers: the network and the leakage were estimated from the same session.

How well it predicts. So every parameter was fitted again on the first 7,400 s of the long session (15 runs, plus the 7-second session of section 2) and frozen, and the 12 later runs were predicted. Nothing is fitted on the held-out part: the thermal state at each launch comes from an observer that has seen the telemetry up to that launch only (tools/ettelem/validate_flip_model.py). The held-out runs include four patterns and two minion counts that the thermal fit had never seen.

  • Up to a few minutes it predicts as well as it fits: for the seven held-out runs that reached 90 °C the median error is 9% and the worst 23% (ones, 199 s for 162 s); six of the seven are within 20%.
  • At ten minutes it runs hot, by 3 to 5 °C on the low-power runs (3.8 °C rms against 2.5 °C in the fit), and, as in the fit, it has the same two runs reaching 90 °C, now earlier (437 and 469 s), when they ended at 87 and 88 °C.
  • The slow stages are not pinned down. Between the half fit and the full fit the fast stages and the leakage barely move (0.10 and 0.05 °C/W at 1.5 and 4 s in both; 24.4 against 23.3 W of leakage at 80 °C), but the slow resistance moves between stages: 0.60 → 0.86 °C/W at 400 s, 0.38 → 0 at 1,000 s and 0 → 0.08 at 2,500 s (the table lists only the stages the full fit kept); the operand-word energy goes from 20 to 15.5 fJ. The telemetry is 140,000 samples but only 27 runs; that is the number that counts for the slow stages. The overnight idle point that anchors them is a single measurement, assumed to share this session's ambient.
  • What does not work at all is letting the model run free for the whole four hours: with a loop gain of 0.95, a 1% error in leakage grows into degrees, and the simulated die drifts away within the hour. The model predicts minutes ahead from a known state.

9. Custom workloads: structured matrices

A predictor for custom workloads. tools/ettelem/predict_heat.py wraps the three lines of section 8: give it the A and B tiles of a workload (or a pattern name), the number of active minions and a start temperature, and it replays the tiles through the RTL, counts the flips, and prints the switching watts by kind of flip, the board power, the temperature after 10 s, 1 min and 10 min, the time to a cap, and the duty cycle that would hold the start temperature. The matrices below test it.

The point of the model is to price a workload before running it. tools/ettelem/make_tiles.py generates structured 16×16 operand pairs: Hadamard and DCT matrices, the cosine and sine halves of a DFT, butterfly factors and their dense kaleidoscope products (the family that generalises the fast Fourier transform), identity, banded, block-diagonal, triangular, rank-1, circulant and 4-bit quantised matrices, a weights-times-ReLU pair, and a matrix of negative zeros. Their tiles went through the RTL bench and the three lines of section 8, and the predictions were written down at 13:08 on 21 September (docs/reports/data/2026-09-21-horace-aifoundry2/structured_predictions_before.json), before any of them had run. Fourteen then ran twice each under the strict protocol of section 2.

Board power at the launch temperature: predicted from the tiles alone, before the first run, against measured

The last column is the raw whole-degree reading at the end of the run minus the launch temperature, not de-quantised like section 1's rises; differences under a degree are not resolved.

  • Predicted to 0.9 W rms over a 25 W range, and ten of the fourteen to within 0.6 W (eleven within 0.75 W), from the tiles alone.
  • Structure in the values buys nothing. Kaleidoscope products, the DCT, circulant and rank-1 matrices are dense, and they cost what random normal costs (63 to 64 W): their mantissa bits are just as busy. The Hadamard matrix costs 50 W, like random signs: only the sign and the adder move.
  • Structure in the zeros buys a lot. A pair of butterfly factors (two nonzeros per row, so 64 of 4,096 products survive the gating) costs 39.3 W, the identity 38.5 W, tridiagonal 40.2 W, 4×4 blocks 41.1 W, upper-triangular 44.4 W. The ReLU pair, random weights times half-zero activations, gates half its products and saves 11 of random data's 27.6 W over idle (random normal was rerun in the same 7 s session as the structured matrices).
  • Negative zero is not zero. The gating looks for the all-zero bit pattern, so a matrix of −0.0 clocks every register: 46.7 W, the same as ones, against 38.2 W for +0.0 (also rerun in that session). The model said 47.2 W. In practice: mask with a select, not by multiplying by zero, because x · 0 is −0.0 for every negative x.
  • The worst miss is the DFT pair, 2.8 W low. Its products cancel almost exactly, which works the normaliser harder than any pattern the energies were fitted on; one energy for every toggle outside the multiplier tree is too coarse there.

And the heat, not just the watts. Six of the structured matrices, with ones and random normal as references, then ran as long runs, one run each, in a separate afternoon session on aifoundry2 (section 7's protocol), with the model frozen as fitted that morning, before they existed on the card. Nothing was fitted on that session: the thermal state at each launch comes from the observer of section 8, from telemetry before the launch only. (The ones run opened the session, so the observer had fewer than three minutes of telemetry before it; it is not in the table.)

Four of the six times to 90 °C are within 3%, and the five-minute run's end temperature is off by 0.2 °C. The DFT pair is wrong by two thirds, for the same reason as its watts: 2.8 W missing out of 13 W of switching costs 47 s on a die this close to runaway. The ReLU pair is a third slow for a 1.2 W miss. Near the card's flip budget the time to a cap is a sensitive function of power, so a watt of model error matters; at full random load it does not (19.5 s predicted, 19.1 s measured). With the first-half model of section 8 instead, the same six come out at 7% in the median and 65% for the DFT pair.

10. A second card, and what transfers

Everything to this point is one card. Of AI Foundry's other two machines, aifoundry1's cards could not be opened when checked, once, on 22 September (the DVFS report, §3); aifoundry3's card works, and it is a useful one: it idled at 51 °C before its session (aifoundry2: 62 °C after one cool night, 73 °C after 20.6 hours idle on 22 September), too cool to hold at 80 °C between runs, so its runs launch at 55 °C; and it stays pinned at 600 MHz, because its firmware reports a TDP of 0 W, so the governor can only step down (The ET-SoC-1's DVFS loop, and the leakage). Running the same strict protocol on it is the strongest out-of-sample test of the power model: different silicon, different heatsink, a launch temperature of 55.8 °C instead of 81.0 °C, and coefficients fitted entirely on the other card.

Switching power over idle, eight operand patterns, two cards. Below: aifoundry3 minus the scaled model, per pattern. Move the scale, or calibrate it on one pattern, and watch the residuals.

The operating point does not explain the gap, and points the other way: aifoundry3 runs its minions at 523 mV against aifoundry2's 518 mV at the same 600 MHz, which by \(P = C_\mathrm{sw}V^{2}f\) predicts about 2% more switching power, not 8% less. Whatever the 8% is — silicon, package, the board regulator's efficiency at that load, or the 25 °C cooler die it ran at — one number captures it.

The leakage half transfers as well. The idle law, fitted here to idle readings from 64 to 88 °C (it also passes through the 62 °C overnight point) and extrapolated 7 to 14 °C below that onto the other card, which idled at 50 to 57 °C, predicts that card's idle power to (the mean of the four temperature bins below) out of 25 W in this session; over that card's 16 sessions the offset is +0.6 W on average (the DVFS report, §5). The offset depends on the split between fixed power and leakage, which the idle data leave open (section 8): across the splits that fit, this session's is 0.2 to 1.2 W.

The °C row is samples from the first s of the session, before any launch. With the idle rule the model was fitted with (samples at least 4.5 s from any launch, 600 MHz, 3 s smoothing, bins of 30 samples or more) only °C remain and the mean offset is W.

What this means if you have more than one of these cards. The shape of the data-dependence — which operand patterns cost what, relative to each other — is a property of the design and moves between cards unchanged. The absolute scale and the idle offset are per-card constants, worth about 8% and 0.7 W. A single random-data matmul on a new card calibrates both, after which the power predictions of sections 3 and 9 apply to it. The heat predictions also need that card's own thermal network, fitted from long runs on it: aifoundry2's comes from one card in one chassis, and aifoundry3's own fit to its 13-minute session already needs more stages; its cooling over minutes was not measured.

11. Method

  • Evidence: sections 1 to 9 rest on aifoundry2's card, one session per experiment; section 10 repeats the 7 s runs on aifoundry3. The text says where a result holds on both cards, gives both values where they differ, and gives the number of runs wherever it is fewer than three. The tests and intervals behind each statement are in the claims-check record.
  • Protocol and checks on aifoundry2 (section 2): four burn-in cycles come first so that the heatsink reaches its periodic state. Patterns then run in shuffled blocks, one run of each per block, and the random patterns get new random tiles in every block: 46 measured runs and 4 burn-in cycles in 58 minutes. Heating and cooling to the launch point took 12 to 120 s (median 63 s), depending on what had run before. Every sample of every run reads 600 MHz and 516 to 518 mV on the die. The thermal network of section 4 puts the die at 80.96 °C (in the sensor's units) at the 46 launch instants, with a spread of 0.11 °C; idle power in the two seconds before launch was 36.29 W with a spread of 0.06 W. No drift over the hour: by block, random normal read 63.38, 63.42, 63.46, 63.25 and 63.49 W and rose 5.16, 5.16, 5.10, 5.15 and 5.17 °C; zeros read 38.25 to 38.32 W in all five. aifoundry3's session (section 10): 20 runs in three blocks in 13 minutes, launched at 55.8 °C.
  • Kernel: hart 0 (the first of each minion's two hardware threads) of each of 1,024 minions repeats TensorFMA32 on one 16×16×16 tile pair held in the L1 scratchpad (4,096 multiply-adds per op, 546 cycles per op for every pattern). FLOPs count one multiply and one add per multiply-add; the rate includes the gaps between launches.
  • Telemetry: tools/ettelem samples board power (10 mW steps, a new value about every 150 ms on aifoundry2 while it samples and about every 255 ms on aifoundry3), the rail averages, the mean minion-shire temperature (whole degrees), clock and core voltage at 10 Hz.
  • Firmware: the governor of section 6 was read in the et-platform source at 353f20e (thermal_pwr_mgmt.c); the cards' own trace strings match an older build (before et-platform commit 60b40c10f, 24 September 2024).
  • Power "at 80 °C": the mean over seconds 1–3 of a run, moved to the start temperature with the measured leakage slope and the temperature in that window. The correction is below 0.5 W for the cool patterns and about 2 W for random data.
  • RTL activity: rtl-sim/fma_toggle (its README lists what is and is not modelled). Counts are per op and per minion, averaged over the seeds the card ran and over two points of a launch (accumulators after 10³ and 4×10⁵ ops).
  • Thermal network: a Foster chain with fixed time constants and non-negative resistances, fitted by least squares to the whole session (board power in, sensor reading out), skipping the first 3 minutes.
  • Long runs: one process per run with the host's --budget raised and --stop-file as the cap: the runner touches the file when the sensor reads 90 °C (or board power 73 W, or telemetry goes stale) and the host stops between two 0.5 s launches. The 10-second etiquette limit was waived by the card's user for these sessions; between runs the runner waits while anyone else has the card open.
  • Flips-to-temperature model: tools/ettelem/flip_thermal_model.py, fitted to the long session up to the cooling change, the strict session and the idle samples of the cool starts, with the overnight idle point (62 °C, 26.7 W) as the anchor for the slowest stage. Per-run predictions start from the network state that the measured power history leaves at launch. tools/ettelem/validate_flip_model.py tests a frozen model on sessions or parts of sessions it was not fitted to, fitting nothing: it estimates the state at each launch causally and reports predicted against measured.
  • Results are not checked for these patterns (random sums are not exact); the same kernel is checked exactly in its integer mode, and the RTL bench checks every multiply-add against software.
