The Horace experiment on the ET-SoC-1

aifoundry2's card, 20 September 2026 · fp32 TensorFMA on all 1,024 minions at a fixed 600 MHz · workloads/sparsity --values, tools/ettelem

Horace He's post "Strangely, Matrix Multiplications on GPUs Run Faster When Given 'Predictable' Data!" (April 2024) found that an A100 multiplies matrices of zeros about 15% faster than random ones (295 against 257 TFLOPS). His explanation: predictable data flips fewer transistors, draws less power, and a power-limited GPU can then clock higher. (The post's direction is that predictable data uses less power, not random data.) On this chip the same physics shows up much more directly. The clock is fixed and the card has power headroom, so speed does not move at all: 546 cycles per operation for every pattern. Power does: 39 W for zeros, 48 W for any constant, 65 W for random values. All of the difference is on the minion-core rail.

1. Result

Board power by operand pattern, corrected to 82 °C (split: minion-core rail, everything else)
  • Zeros are cheapest, as on the GPU. The tensor unit gates a multiply whose operand is zero (the sparsity report measured that), so this is clock gating more than quiet wires.
  • Every constant costs the same. Ones, twos, π (a dense mantissa) and a float with a single set bit all land within 0.5 W. So it is not how many bits are set that costs energy. What costs is bits that change from one operand to the next: with a constant, the multiplier inputs stay the same every cycle and only the accumulator moves.
  • Random values cost the most: +17 W over a constant, +25.5 W over zeros. Uniform [0,1) is 1.9 W below random normal: no sign flips and a narrower exponent range.
  • Half zeros is not half way. The checkerboard (every other element zero) and the 75%-zeros random matrix sit at 41.5 and 41.9 W, close to zeros. A product is gated when either operand is zero, so with both A and B half zero only a quarter of the products run; with 25% dense random in both, about 6%.
  • Only the core rail moves. SRAM and mesh power are the same to 0.1 W for every pattern: the data travels the same wires either way, and the arithmetic is where the switching is.

2. Why no speed-up here

The A100 result needs a power-limited part: lower power lets the GPU hold higher clocks. This card ran all patterns at a fixed 600 MHz, and the clock stayed put even as random data took it to 69 W against a 65 W TDP setting and 90 °C. So the ET-SoC-1 shows the cause (data-dependent switching power) without the GPU's effect (data-dependent speed). At iso-power the conclusion flips into Horace's: a DVFS governor pinned at a power limit would clock the zeros case higher.

3. Temperature is a confound, and a finding

Power rises with die temperature at the same work: 0.78 W per °C on the board, 0.38 W per °C on the minion rail (leakage). Over the five-minute run the die went from 79 to 90 °C, which alone is worth 8 W. The patterns were therefore run in three rounds in rotated order, and the numbers above come from a least-squares fit of pattern plus a temperature term (residual 0.25 W rms over 22 runs; the third round was cut short, so eight patterns have two runs and ones and onebit have three). Any power comparison on this card needs the same care.

Every run: board power against die temperature, coloured by pattern group

4. Method

  • Kernel: hart 0 of each of 1,024 minions runs back-to-back TensorFMA ops on one 16×16×16 fp32 tile pair held in the L1 scratchpad, 6 s per pattern (each process under 10 s of card time). The host fills both A and B with the pattern.
  • Power: tools/ettelem samples board power and the service processor's rail averages at 10 Hz; each run uses the mean over its last 3 s, because the rail averages lag by about 2 s.
  • Energy per multiply-add: 1,024 minions × 600 MHz / 546 cycles × 4,096 multiply-adds per op = 4.6 × 10¹² per second; 25.5 W / that = 5.5 pJ between random and zeros, and 1.8 pJ between a constant and zeros.
  • Results are not checked for these patterns (random sums are not exact); the same kernel is checked exactly in its integer mode.
