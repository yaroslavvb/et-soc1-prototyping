1. The answer

Random fp32 data makes about 13.5 of the 32 bits of each operand change from one multiply-add to the next; zeros make none change. Every changed bit drives a cascade of wires through the multiplier's Wallace tree, and every wire that moves charges a capacitance. That is the whole mechanism: the card's clock governor regulates board power to a cap, random data draws more power for the same arithmetic, so the governor gives it a lower clock, and a lower clock is fewer FLOPS. The numbers below are the same difference restated at each depth.

On a cool die, in the first eight seconds, the card does on zeros against on random fp32 — faster. Once it is hot and the thermal test takes over, zeros hold 800 MHz while random data sits on the governor's 600 MHz floor, and the gap becomes . At 800 MHz the board draws on zeros and on random data. The rungs below that are each measured in their own experiment rather than divided out of it: a random multiply-add costs over idle against on zeros, and one flipped bit of an operand costs at the board. They do agree where they can be made to meet — the operand-dependent half of the per-multiply-add budget, , spread over the operand bits a random pair changes, is per bit, inside that band — but no rung here is a quotient of the one above it, and §5 shows why the per-bit prices cannot simply be added up. Underneath that bit, a bottom-up count on an open synthesis of the same RTL finds physical net toggles per random multiply-add; the gates they charge hold transistor fins per signal net; and one minimum transistor's gate takes — about electrons across the 0.517 V rail, which is times Landauer's floor at the die's own temperature.

4. Back up the ladder: predicting the speed gap from below

A chain is only worth having if it runs in both directions. Starting from board power and the governor's own cap, the model predicts the clock each pattern settles at, and therefore the TFLOPS gap it produces.

The burst gap is a real prediction and it lands. The model predicts against a measurement of , depending on which of four published readings you take — first-eight-second TFLOPS, whole-run TFLOPS, burst clocks, or mean clock after the first 1.5 s. The point estimate sits inside that spread; the model cannot tell the four readings apart. Two inputs set how sharp it can be: the cap (64 W would give 1.159, 67 W would give 1.127) and the drift of the price list between cards, which carried as a band gives .

The cap axis is measured now, not extrapolated. Both tiles above read the governor at one factory setting when this page was written. E69 moved the cap on 28 October and the extrapolated table holds: . All-ones operands throttle to ; random operands read . So the burst prediction is no longer a prediction against a single point.

The sustained gap is not a prediction at all, and saying so is the honest result. Once the thermal test takes over, zeros clamp to the top of the clock range and random data clamps to the bottom, so the predicted ratio is 800/600 = 1.333 for any cap in a window — and of the candidate caps on the record sit inside it. The measurement is the same number by the same construction. What the chain does predict here is ordinal, and it is still a real claim: random data reaches the governor's floor and zeros does not.

Conventions, and the two places it is easy to go wrong

A rise is not a toggle. A capacitance C takes C·V² from the rail when its net rises and nothing when it falls, so a toggle — the per-edge average — costs ½C·V². Every single-edge figure on this page is per rise; per-toggle figures are half of it and are labelled where they appear. A table that mixes the two is out by exactly a factor of two, which is large enough to matter and small enough to miss.

An electron count from a board figure is a convention. Board power is not delivered at the die's rail, so dividing a board energy by 0.517 V gives a number of electrons that is useful for comparison and is not a count of anything. Every electron figure here uses the same 0.517 V; the cards that produced these measurements ran at 521.9 mV, so a count at their own rail would be 0.9% lower.

Temperature. Every SPICE number is at the kit's 25 °C corner. The die temperature enters only at Landauer's floor, where it is stated.
