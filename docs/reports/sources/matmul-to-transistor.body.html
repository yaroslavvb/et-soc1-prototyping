From matmul speed to one transistor switch

Tuesday 27 October 2026 · the ET-SoC-1 at AI Foundry · every number below is a committed measurement or a model built on one, in the repository

Horace He's observation is that a matrix multiply runs faster on zeros than on random numbers, although it does exactly the same arithmetic. On this card it does: 15% faster in the first seconds, 33% faster once the die is hot. Nothing about the instructions changed — only how many wires inside the chip had to move. This page follows that one difference all the way down: from TFLOPS to the governor, to watts at the board, to picojoules per multiply-add, to femtojoules per flipped bit of an operand, to the toggles of individual wires, to the gates they charge, to one transistor's gate, to the electrons in it and to the thermodynamic floor underneath. Nine steps. Six of them are measured on silicon, three are modelled, and the gap that is left is shown rather than hidden.

1. The answer

Random fp32 data makes about 13.5 of the 32 bits of each operand change from one multiply-add to the next; zeros make none change. Every changed bit drives a cascade of wires through the multiplier's Wallace tree, and every wire that moves charges a capacitance. That is the whole mechanism: the card's clock governor regulates board power to a cap, random data draws more power for the same arithmetic, so the governor gives it a lower clock, and a lower clock is fewer FLOPS. The numbers below are the same difference restated at each depth.

On a cool die, in the first eight seconds, the card does on zeros against on random fp32 — faster. Once it is hot and the thermal test takes over, zeros hold 800 MHz while random data sits on the governor's 600 MHz floor, and the gap becomes . At 800 MHz the board draws on zeros and on random data. The rungs below that are each measured in their own experiment rather than divided out of it: a random multiply-add costs over idle against on zeros, and one flipped bit of an operand costs at the board. They do agree where they can be made to meet — the operand-dependent half of the per-multiply-add budget, , spread over the operand bits a random pair changes, is per bit, inside that band — but no rung here is a quotient of the one above it, and §5 shows why the per-bit prices cannot simply be added up. Underneath that bit, a bottom-up count on an open synthesis of the same RTL finds physical net toggles per random multiply-add; the gates they charge hold transistor fins per signal net; and one minimum transistor's gate takes — about electrons across the 0.517 V rail, which is times Landauer's floor at the die's own temperature.

The one rule that does not hold. If each bit had a fixed price you could add them: 13.5 changed bits in a random operand, times the price list, would give the energy of a random word. It does not. Summed naively over both operands the price list predicts where the card draws — too much. Why the remainder is expected, and what would shrink it. The bottom-up figure is a floor twice over — three, until 27 October 2026. One, half the design's capacitance is unpriced — of the farads sit on nets that the clock-tree build and the timing repair created after the netlist was simulated. Two, the ledger covers one lane's arithmetic, while the board also pays for the register file's read ports, the operand bus, the result path and leakage. The third reason is withdrawn: this page said the between-cell wire is a global-route estimate with no coupling term, because detailed routing never closed. Routing has now closed, and the estimate was never coupling-free — it runs 20–23% above the extraction, so extracting the wire moves 0.137 pJ out of “accounted” and widens this remainder to 3.97 pJ. The remainder is not a measurement error; it is the parts of the chip this count does not yet reach.

4. Back up the ladder: predicting the speed gap from below

A chain is only worth having if it runs in both directions. Starting from board power and the governor's own cap, the model predicts the clock each pattern settles at, and therefore the TFLOPS gap it produces.

The burst gap is a real prediction and it lands. The model predicts against a measurement of , depending on which of four published readings you take — first-eight-second TFLOPS, whole-run TFLOPS, burst clocks, or mean clock after the first 1.5 s. The point estimate sits inside that spread; the model cannot tell the four readings apart. Two inputs set how sharp it can be: the cap (64 W would give 1.159, 67 W would give 1.127) and the drift of the price list between cards, which carried as a band gives .

The sustained gap is not a prediction at all, and saying so is the honest result. Once the thermal test takes over, zeros clamp to the top of the clock range and random data clamps to the bottom, so the predicted ratio is 800/600 = 1.333 for any cap in a window — and of the candidate caps on the record sit inside it. The measurement is the same number by the same construction. What the chain does predict here is ordinal, and it is still a real claim: random data reaches the governor's floor and zeros does not.

5. What is still open

In the order that closing them would most change the picture.

Waits on the card's owner

The knob test: move the cap and see whether the gap moves with it

Everything in §1 and §2 says the governor's power cap causes the speed gap. That is read from the governor's own log, which is strong evidence, but it is still observational: the cap has never actually been changed. Raising the TDP, then the thermal threshold, on aifoundry2 and re-running the same four patterns would turn the whole chain's top rung from a correlation into a controlled result — and it would test the model's own extrapolated 35–85 W cap table at the same time. It costs about half an hour of card time. It is not an agent's call to make: the lab's rule is that nobody sets a TDP, threshold or clock on a shared card. aifoundry3's governor is latched by a boot-time TDP of 0 W and never steps, and aifoundry1's card 1 never reaches 800 MHz, so it is aifoundry2 or nowhere.

Cheap, written, not run

The saturation ladder: how many bits, rather than which bits

The single-bit price list over-predicts a whole random word by a factor of about two, and nothing in the record separates the two candidate causes — that simultaneous flips share wires, or that the price of a flip depends on what else is flipping. A ladder in the number of simultaneously flipping positions (2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12) on one base, against the existing rungs, settles it. The masks are built and committed. About 20 minutes of card time.

Measured tonight, needs one more pass

The leakage slope, which is the largest uncertainty in every per-bit price

Each run's power is corrected for the die temperature it ran at, using a leakage slope. The registered estimator fits that slope inside each run's own two-second window, while the kernel is running, so it reads a busy transient rather than idle leakage — and it returned 1.508 W/°C. An overnight idle heat-and-cool cycle measured this card's real idle leakage directly at , five times smaller, in agreement with three other estimates. Every price on this page is therefore published as a band. Re-running the reduction with the measured slope — as a labelled sensitivity, not as a replacement for the registered scoring — is a morning's desk work.

Structural

The three things that keep the bottom-up count a floor

Detailed routing has never closed in the open flow, so there is no extracted parasitic file, no coupling capacitance and no measured glitch factor. Half the design's capacitance was created after the point the gate-level simulation saw. And the A side of the multiplier has no multi-bit ladder, so the carry-over from one operand to the other is a choice between two defensible numbers that moves the prediction by 6%.

Beyond this unit

Memory, and the instructions that trap

No flip ladder has run against a memory path. A DRAM tensor load costs 94–134 pJ per byte against a multiply-add's 5.8 pJ, so for most real work this chain prices the small half. Thirteen instructions — divide, square root, reciprocal square root, sine, the integer divides — trap in user mode and have no energy figure at all, and they are the normalisation half of a real kernel.

Conventions, and the two places it is easy to go wrong

A rise is not a toggle. A capacitance C takes C·V² from the rail when its net rises and nothing when it falls, so a toggle — the per-edge average — costs ½C·V². Every single-edge figure on this page is per rise; per-toggle figures are half of it and are labelled where they appear. A table that mixes the two is out by exactly a factor of two, which is large enough to matter and small enough to miss.

An electron count from a board figure is a convention. Board power is not delivered at the die's rail, so dividing a board energy by 0.517 V gives a number of electrons that is useful for comparison and is not a count of anything. Every electron figure here uses the same 0.517 V; the cards that produced these measurements ran at 521.9 mV, so a count at their own rail would be 0.9% lower.

Temperature. Every SPICE number is at the kit's 25 °C corner. The die temperature enters only at Landauer's floor, where it is stated.
