One hot line stops a shire

Two ET-SoC-1 cards, 22 September 2026 · workloads/nocbench --test hotline, a new many-to-one probe · following up a benchmark result Ivan reported in Discord · part of the measurement reports

Ivan put one global atomic counter in shire 0's scratchpad, had all 32 shires hammer it, and found that shire 0 got 6% of its fair share and finished only after the other 31. The obvious reading is that the shire cache short-changes whoever owns the line. It does not. The atomic is shared out to within one part in a thousand, including with the shire that hosts it. What that shire loses is something else entirely: its own memory path, which stops — not slows, stops — for as long as the hammering lasts. The switch is sharp, it takes fewer than one shire's worth of attackers to flip, and it is identical on both cards we have.

1. The atomic itself is fair

Every minion in the launch hammers one 4-byte word with amoaddg.w. All of them meet at a chip-wide barrier first, so the window is the same window for everyone, and each one reports how many atomics it completed inside it. A shire's share is its count divided by what an even split would give.

Per-shire share of one contended atomic, 1,024 minions, line homed in shire 0

Moving the line's home to shire 7, 15 or 31 changes nothing, so shire 0 is not special. The one case where shares do spread out is the opposite of starvation: with a single minion per shire the bank is not saturated, latency decides, and the host shire comes first because its request has no mesh to cross.

2. What contention costs, and what it does not

One line is worth 32 in nothing but placement. The same 1,024 minions running the same instruction against 32 lines, one per shire, do 32 times the work:

Ten cycles per atomic is the shire cache retiring one request at a time; the repository's own note that a sub-bank accepts a request every two cycles puts a global atomic at about five sub-bank slots. The aggregate of about 60 million atomics a second matches the figure Ivan quoted, which settles that it was an aggregate and not a per-shire number.

3. The thing that actually starves

So we changed what the host shire does. Instead of joining the atomic, its 32 minions stream their own memory — 64-byte-strided loads over a private slice, either of their shire's scratchpad or of DRAM — while the other 31 shires hammer a line that lives in that same shire cache. The baseline is the identical loop with nobody else launched.

These are not slow-downs, they are stops. The host shire's count is the same number whether the window is 5 milliseconds or 100:

Twelve loads per minion get through while the mesh traffic ramps up, and then nothing — through six million remote atomics. The 32 minions of that shire, a full thirty-second of the chip, are stalled in a load instruction that does not return. Nothing reports an error; the kernel finishes when the hammering stops.

4. The threshold is the bank going saturated

It takes one other shire. Holding everything else fixed and varying how many of that one shire's minions join in:

Host shire's own memory throughput against the rate of remote atomics

There is no gradual degradation. Up to twenty remote requesters the host shire is within a percent of untouched, because each remote atomic takes about 216 cycles to make its round trip and twenty of them in flight still ask for less than the bank can retire. At twenty-four the measured cost reaches 10.0 cycles per atomic — the bank's floor, the queue full — and the host shire's own requests stop getting resources.

$$\frac{N_\text{remote}}{t_\text{round trip}} \;<\; \frac{1}{10\ \text{cycles}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{the host shire is unaffected}$$

5. Esperanto knew, and wrote down why the fix does not work

This is not a discovery so much as a demonstration. Two errata describe it, and both are marked Postponed:

That answers both of the questions worth sending back. Does setting l3_yield restore the host's share? The erratum says in as many words that it does not, for exactly this case — same address, neighbourhood against mesh — and the second erratum adds that the yield has no sub-bank granularity even when the addresses differ. Does it hold when the line is DRAM-backed rather than in scratchpad? Yes, and slightly worse: 192 operations instead of 384. The erratum's title says same SCP address, but the behaviour is not specific to the scratchpad. Any address whose home is that shire cache will do it.

Worth noting what Esperanto rated the impact: Low. It would have to be a pretty consistent and repeating pattern. A shared counter, a lock, a flag or a queue head is a pretty consistent and repeating pattern.

The erratum's advice is to slow the other shires down. It works, and near the knee it is nearly free — the bank was already saturated, so the first thing pacing takes away is queueing, not throughput:

A thousand minions stalled on a saturated line cost the card about a watt and a half over idle, which is the clearest possible statement that waiting is cheap and the loss is throughput, not power. Per operation the contended line costs times the energy of the same atomic spread over 32 lines.

8. What to do instead

  • One global atomic per shire, never per minion. At 32 participants the serialisation is 320 cycles against a chip-wide barrier we measure at 4,997 cycles — six percent, invisible. At 1,024 it is 10,240 cycles, twice the whole barrier, and it takes the hosting shire down with it.
  • Keep hot shared lines out of shires that compute. The cost is not paid by the code touching the line. It is paid by whatever else lives in that shire, and it is total.
  • If you must, pace it. Ten thousand cycles between atomics, about 17 microseconds, gives the host shire back half its memory path and costs the hammering shires four percent.
  • Spread instead of sharing when you can. One line per shire is the same instruction at 32 times the rate and a seventeenth of the energy per operation.

9. Method, and what is not established

  • The probe is NB_HOTLINE in workloads/nocbench, new for this. Every participant warms up, meets at a chip-wide barrier, then loops until a cycle deadline, counting completed operations. Counting completions inside a common window is what makes "share" mean anything; a fixed-count race would confound share with finishing order.
  • The home shire of a DRAM line is PA[10:6], so the probe allocates a 2 KB-aligned region and uses the line at offset s×64. Scratchpad lines use the PRM's format 0, shire ID in bits [29:23]. The region is verified aligned at launch, and the 32-fold speed-up of one line per shire is the check that the address really does select the bank.
  • A global atomic cannot take the local path at all. Addressed through the self ID 0x7F, amoaddg raises a kernel bus error. That is why section 1 comes out fair: from the host shire the atomic leaves through the same L3-slave port as everyone else's, so the arbitration that ranks the two classes never applies to it. The starvation in section 3 needs an ordinary load or store, which is what a computing shire issues.
  • Everything is measured on aifoundry2 and repeated on aifoundry3. The two cards agree to the individual operation: 384 and 384, 0.02% and 0.02%, 10.0 and 10.0 cycles. Raw data in docs/reports/data/2026-09-22-hotline-aifoundry2/ and -aifoundry3/, one JSON line per configuration.
  • Not established: why Ivan's number was 6% rather than either of ours. Our reading is that his shire 0 did something local as well as the atomic — a poll, a flag read, the "am I last?" check a barrier makes — and that the local part is what was starved; but we did not run his code. We also did not set l3_yield: it is a shire-cache configuration register on a shared lab card, and the erratum says it would not help here anyway. And the card's thermal telemetry is one chip-wide mean, so the spatial question — whether a hot line makes its shire measurably hotter — cannot be answered with this instrument.
